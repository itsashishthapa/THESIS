\newcommand{\ABSTRACT}{
Energy utility systems operate under dynamic conditions in which electricity
prices, renewable generation, storage states, and interacting process
variables affect operating cost, efficiency, reliability, and constraint
satisfaction. Improving their operation can also support renewable-energy
integration and reduce energy-related carbon emissions. Their setpoints are
conventionally determined using mathematical optimization, which depends on
accurate system models and can become computationally demanding as system
complexity increases. Time-varying inputs, renewable-energy uncertainty, and
model--plant mismatch further motivate the investigation of data-driven
alternatives. This thesis therefore evaluates whether reinforcement learning
can provide effective setpoints with low online computation time for two
systems of different complexity: a battery energy storage system and the
electrified steam-generation utility system of a cookie production factory.

Both problems are formulated as finite-horizon Markov decision processes, and actor-critic agents are trained using Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) and Twin Delayed Deep
Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3). Their schedules are compared with
mathematical optimization using identical input data and are assessed in terms
of economic objective, feasibility, and online computation time.

For the battery system, TD3 closely approached the economic performance of
mathematical optimization while generating schedules approximately
$68\times$ faster. For the more complex cookie factory, SAC and TD3 achieved
mean absolute relative objective gaps of 6.31\% and 5.66\%, respectively,
while generating setpoints approximately $534\times$ and $645\times$ faster
than mathematical optimization. Although SAC had the larger economic gap, it
produced smaller $F_4$-coupling residuals, heat-flow-bound violations, and
terminal cyclic deviations, whereas TD3 produced smaller grid-power
violations. Both cookie-factory policies produced constraint violations,
whereas mathematical optimization remained feasible.

The results show that trained reinforcement-learning policies can generate
economically meaningful setpoints with very low online computation time after
offline training, although some constraint violations remained in the more
complex cookie-factory model.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{Section1}

Modern energy utility systems supply the electricity and heat needed to sustain industrial production and other essential activities. Their efficient operation is important for meeting energy demand while limiting operating costs and environmental impacts. Key objectives of industrial energy management include reducing operating costs and CO$_2$ emissions, improving energy efficiency, and integrating renewable energy sources. Achieving these objectives requires coordinated decisions about energy conversion, storage, and supply while satisfying the requirements of the processes being served.

These operating decisions are shaped by dynamic conditions and multiple sources of uncertainty. Electricity prices vary over time, renewable generation is weather dependent, and storage systems couple current decisions with future operating flexibility \cite{khalid2024smart}. At the same time, industrial energy systems often exhibit nonlinear component behavior and include operating and coupling constraints that must be respected at every time step. The resulting setpoint selection problem is therefore not only an economic problem, but also a constrained sequential decision-making problem.

In many engineering applications, such setpoints are determined using mathematical optimization. This approach is attractive because physical constraints and objective functions can be formulated explicitly, and mature solvers can compute high-quality solutions for well-defined models. For energy systems, nonlinear programming methods such as IPOPT \cite{wachter2006implementation} are commonly used when component models, storage dynamics, and operational constraints are nonlinear \cite{walden2023nonlinear}. However, deterministic global optimization can become computationally demanding for large-scale or nonconvex problems, making it difficult to obtain a globally optimal solution within a practical time limit \cite{lin2012deterministic}. Stochastic global optimization methods can also require many objective-function evaluations and exhibit slow convergence, with computational cost increasing as the problem dimension grows \cite{zhigljavsky2008stochastic}. In industrial energy management, forecast uncertainty, optimization-horizon length, and computation time per rolling-horizon iteration are additional factors that influence system performance \cite{kyriakidis2025energy}.

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a different perspective. Unlike classical control and optimization methods that solve a trajectory-optimization problem from scratch at each operating horizon, RL agents learn a policy or action-value function gradually through interactive experience. Once trained, this learned representation enables rapid selection of actions from the current observation \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. This makes RL attractive for real-time or repeated decision-making problems, especially when the system is complex, nonlinear, and operates in a changing environment \cite{pilling2025rl}. Nevertheless, RL does not automatically guarantee feasibility or optimality. For engineering use, a learned policy must be evaluated not only by its reward, but also by its operating cost, constraint satisfaction, and computation time relative to established optimization methods.

This thesis investigates whether RL can be used for optimal setpoint determination in energy utility systems of different complexity. Two case studies are considered to examine how the number of control variables, nonlinear component behavior, and coupling between operational constraints affect the applicability of RL. The first is a battery energy storage system (BESS), which represents a comparatively simple storage-control problem with a single charging or discharging command, state-of-charge dynamics, and time-varying electricity prices. The second is the electrified steam-generation utility system of a cookie production factory, incorporating wind power and thermal energy storage. Based on the benchmark systems described by Walden et al. and Kyriakidis et al. \cite{walden2023nonlinear,kyriakidis2024hybrid}, this case has greater complexity because it combines multiple continuous control variables, nonlinear heat-pump and heat-transfer behavior, and coupled mass-flow, temperature, and storage constraints. Comparing the two cases allows the benefits and limitations of RL to be assessed across a simpler storage system and a more complex industrial energy utility system.

The central research question of this thesis is:
\begin{quote}
\itshape Can reinforcement learning determine feasible and cost-effective operating setpoints for energy utility systems of varying complexity?
\end{quote}
This question is addressed through the following subquestions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item How can the setpoint optimization problem be formulated as a Markov decision process with suitable states, actions, and rewards?
    \item Can deep reinforcement-learning policies learn continuous setpoint decisions that respect the system dynamics and operational constraints of the two systems?
    \item How does the learned policy compare with mathematical optimization in terms of operating cost, constraint satisfaction, and computation time?
    \item Under which conditions does RL offer a practical advantage, and where do classical optimization methods remain superior?
\end{itemize}

For each case study, the setpoint optimization problem is first formulated as a Markov decision process (MDP). Deep neural networks are used to parameterize the RL policies that map system observations to continuous setpoint decisions. The resulting policies are evaluated through deterministic rollouts in reinforcement-learning environments, and the resulting schedules are compared with mathematical optimization. The comparison is based on total grid-electricity cost, constraint violations, and computation time. This separation is important because a policy with high cumulative reward is only useful if it remains feasible and economically meaningful under the original engineering objective.

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows: Section~\ref{sec:theory_background} establishes the theoretical foundations of setpoint optimization and reinforcement learning, and introduces mathematical optimization as the benchmark used to evaluate the RL policies. It also describes the deep RL methods considered in this work. Section~\ref{sec:use_case} introduces the two case studies: a battery energy storage system and a nonlinear cookie production factory. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} presents the methodological framework, covering the MDP formulation, environment design, policy-training procedure, optimization baselines, and evaluation metrics. Section~\ref{sec:results_discussion} reports and discusses the experimental results, with particular emphasis on the comparison between RL policies and mathematical optimization. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion_future_work} summarizes the main findings, identifies the limitations of the study, and outlines directions for future work.

\section{Theory and Background}
\label{sec:theory_background}

This section provides the theoretical basis for the setpoint optimization problem studied in this thesis. The central question is how operating setpoints in energy utility systems can be selected over time so that costs are reduced while physical and operational limits are respected. The same problem can be viewed from two perspectives: as a model-based constrained optimization problem and as a sequential decision-making problem that can be addressed with reinforcement learning. The section first introduces setpoint optimization in energy utility systems and its formulation as a constrained mathematical optimization problem. It then presents the fundamental concepts of RL and the MDP formulation \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}, followed by descriptions of the TD3 \cite{fujimoto2018addressing} and SAC \cite{haarnoja2018soft} algorithms for continuous control evaluated in this work.

Here, RL is not treated as a substitute for physical constraints. Instead, the setpoint-optimization problem is formulated as an MDP, with feasibility enforced where possible through action bounds, state-dependent mappings, and system dynamics, and otherwise discouraged through reward penalties. This distinction is important when comparing RL with mathematical optimization, because a low-cost policy is only useful if its decisions remain feasible.

\subsection{Setpoint Optimization in Energy Utility Systems}

Energy utility systems are operated by choosing setpoints that determine how components such as storage units, heat pumps, valves, and bypasses are controlled. A setpoint describes the desired value of a control variable. In the systems considered here, this may be the charging or discharging power of a battery, the bypass variable $\beta_1$, or a mass flow rate. These choices are not just technical details; they directly affect operating cost, energy efficiency, constraint satisfaction, and overall system reliability and lifetime \cite{tran2025safe}.

Setpoint determination is also inherently sequential. The best operating point at one time step cannot be chosen independently of what happened before or what may be needed later. Storage systems make this especially clear. Charging a battery or a thermal energy storage system can increase future flexibility, but it may also increase the current electricity cost. Discharging can reduce current grid consumption or generate sale revenue, but it leaves less stored energy for later. Good setpoint decisions must therefore account for inter-temporal dependencies and physical operating limits \cite{walden2023nonlinear}. Each action affects both the immediate economic outcome and the feasible decisions available at later time steps; consequently, its quality must be evaluated over the full planning horizon rather than solely at the current time step \cite{takam2025stochastic}.

In practical operation, the controller also has to react to external signals such as electricity prices, renewable generation, production demand, and ambient conditions. Since these signals change over time, fixed rule-based strategies can become inefficient, and the operation can be formulated as a finite-horizon decision problem with time-varying boundary conditions \cite{meng2025online,pilling2025rl}. This motivates two formulations of the same setpoint determination task: constrained finite-horizon optimization and reinforcement learning.

\subsection{Mathematical Optimization}

Mathematical optimization provides a natural way to describe setpoint selection. In general, optimization means selecting the best decision from a set of feasible alternatives according to a defined objective. For energy systems, the objective is often to minimize operating costs, energy consumption, or CO$_2$ emissions, while the feasible set is determined by physical equations and operational limits \cite{walden2023nonlinear,kyriakidis2025energy}. A constrained optimization problem can be written as \cite{nocedal2006numerical}:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \in \Omega} f(x) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad h(x) = 0,\; g(x) \le 0,
\label{eq:general_constrained_optimization}
\end{equation}
Here, $x \in \Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ denotes the decision variables, $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is the objective function, $h : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^p$ represents equality constraints, and $g : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^q$ represents inequality constraints. The solution $x^\star$ is optimal if it minimizes the objective while satisfying all constraints.

For finite-horizon setpoint optimization, the decision vector contains the control actions for several time steps. The objective is usually the accumulated operating cost over the horizon. The constraints describe system dynamics, energy balances, storage bounds, and terminal requirements. This time-coupled structure is important because a state such as battery state of charge or thermal storage temperature depends on previous decisions.

The difficulty of an optimization problem depends strongly on its mathematical structure. Linear and mixed-integer linear formulations are widely used in energy-system applications because simplified models can often be solved reliably. However, realistic energy conversion systems frequently include nonlinear component behavior, continuous control variables, and coupled dynamic constraints. Such systems lead to nonlinear programming (NLP) formulations, which are generally harder to solve and may contain local optima \cite{nocedal2006numerical}. As the number of time steps, decision variables, nonlinear component models, and coupling constraints increases, the optimization problem generally becomes larger and more difficult to solve within a short operational time window \cite{kyriakidis2024hybrid}.

Another important distinction is whether the problem is deterministic or stochastic. In deterministic optimization, all relevant parameters, such as electricity prices, wind availability, and demand, are assumed to be known over the optimization horizon. Stochastic optimization represents uncertain quantities through random variables or scenarios. This is closer to real operation, but it also increases model size and computational complexity.

Nonlinear constrained problems are usually solved with dedicated numerical solvers. Interior-point methods are common in this setting. Wächter and Biegler introduce IPOPT as an open-source solver for large-scale nonlinear programming that uses a primal-dual interior-point method with a filter line-search strategy \cite{wachter2006implementation}.

More advanced hybrid optimization methods, such as Bayesian Optimization-Interior Point OPTimizer (BO-IPOPT), have been developed for complex nonlinear, nonconvex, and constrained optimization problems. BO-IPOPT combines the global exploration capability of Bayesian optimization with the efficient local convergence and constraint handling of IPOPT. The method has been evaluated for the optimal operation of an industrial energy conversion system and subsequently enhanced for constrained problems of varying dimensionality, multimodality, and nonlinearity \cite{kyriakidis2024hybrid,kyriakidis2025enhanced}. In this thesis, IPOPT is used for mathematical optimization because of its wide applicability to nonlinear optimization problems. The evaluation of BO-IPOPT as an additional global optimization method is left for future work.

To illustrate the interior-point principle, consider the general problem in Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_constrained_optimization}. Introducing slack variables converts the inequality constraints into $g(x)+s=0$, where $s\geq 0$. Here, $s=(s_1,\ldots,s_q)^\top$, and $s_j$ is the slack variable associated with the $j$th inequality $g_j(x)\leq 0$. A logarithmic barrier with parameter $\mu>0$ restricts the iterates to the interior, $s>0$, and leads to the subproblem
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{x,s}\quad & f(x) - \mu\sum_{j=1}^{q}\ln(s_j) \\
\text{s.t.}\quad & h(x)=0, \qquad g(x)+s=0, \qquad s>0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ipopt_barrier_problem}
\end{equation}
The logarithmic barrier prevents the iterates from reaching the constraint boundary. IPOPT solves a sequence of such subproblems using a primal-dual Newton method. With equality multipliers $y$ and inequality multipliers $z$, the perturbed Karush-Kuhn-Tucker(KKT) conditions are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\nabla f(x)+J_h(x)^\top y+J_g(x)^\top z &= 0, \\
h(x) &= 0, \qquad g(x)+s=0, \\
S z &= \mu\mathbf{1}, \qquad s>0,\; z>0,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ipopt_kkt}
\end{equation}
where $J_h$ and $J_g$ are the Jacobians of the equality and inequality constraints, respectively, and $S=\operatorname{diag}(s)$. As $\mu$ is reduced towards zero, the perturbed complementarity condition $Sz=\mu\mathbf{1}$ approaches the KKT complementarity condition of the original constrained problem \cite{wachter2006implementation}. 

Nocedal and Wright note that the performance of nonlinear optimization algorithms depends on formulation quality, particularly the constraint structure, variable scaling, and initial point \cite{nocedal2006numerical}.

These considerations motivate the investigation of reinforcement learning as a complementary approach to model-based optimization. The following section introduces its fundamental principles and its formulation for setpoint optimization.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning}

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a different view of the same setpoint problem. Instead of solving an optimization problem from scratch at every decision time, an agent learns from repeated interaction with an environment. At each time step, the agent observes the current state, selects an action, receives a reward, and observes the next state. Over time, it adapts its policy so that actions leading to high long-term reward become more likely \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.

\begin{figure}[!tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/markov_diagram_v2.pdf}
    \caption{Interaction loop between the reinforcement-learning agent and the environment in a Markov decision process \cite{ebatllep2020markov}.}
    \label{fig:rl_interaction_loop}
\end{figure}

Formally, RL problems are commonly modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by the tuple \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}
\[
\left(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A}, p, r, \gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}\right),
\]
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{X}$ is the set of states representing the system,
    \item $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of actions,
    \item $p(x' \mid x,a)$ defines the transition dynamics,
    \item $r(x,a)$ is the reward function,
    \item $\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}} \in [0,1]$ is the discount factor.
\end{itemize}

Under the Markov property, the current state contains all information required to predict the next state, given the selected action. A policy $\pi(a \mid x)$ describes how the agent selects actions. To evaluate a decision, RL considers both its immediate reward and the rewards obtained thereafter. For a finite horizon of $N$ decision steps, this quantity, called the return, is
\[
G_t = \sum_{i=0}^{N-t-1} \gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}^i r(x_{t+i},a_{t+i}),
\]
and the learning objective is to find a policy that maximizes the expected return. The value function and action-value function of a policy $\pi$ are defined as
\[
v_t^\pi(x) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[G_t \mid X_t=x\right],
\qquad
q_t^\pi(x,a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[G_t \mid X_t=x, A_t=a\right].
\]
The value function estimates the expected return from a state, whereas the action-value function estimates the expected return from a state-action pair. These estimates allow the agent to consider the future consequences of a decision, not only its immediate reward.

For a finite-horizon problem, the optimal action-value function can be written recursively as
\[
q_t^\star(x,a)
= r(x,a)
+ \gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}\,
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
v_{t+1}^\star(x')
\mid x,a
\right].
\]
Here, $x'$ denotes the next state. Rather than calculating this expectation from a known system model, RL learns an approximation of the action-value function from sampled transitions $(x_t,a_t,r_t,x_{t+1})$. The policy is then improved to select actions with higher estimated long-term return. This illustrates the central idea of RL: actions are evaluated not only by their immediate reward, but also by their expected future consequences \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.

Classical dynamic programming can solve MDPs through backward recursion. However, this approach becomes impractical when the state or action space grows, a difficulty known as the curse of dimensionality \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. Realistic energy systems often have continuous states, continuous actions, and long horizons, so exact dynamic-programming solutions are often computationally infeasible.

RL addresses this limitation by learning approximate policies from interaction data. A trained policy can then compute an action directly from the current observation. In model-free RL, the learning algorithm does not require an explicit analytical transition model for planning, although training still requires interaction data. In engineering applications, this data is usually generated with a simulator or digital model, so the quality of the learned policy depends strongly on the quality of the training environment.

To place the algorithms used in this thesis in context, it is helpful to briefly classify the main families of RL algorithms. RL algorithms can be classified along several complementary dimensions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Model-based and model-free methods: model-based methods learn or use a model of the environment dynamics for planning. Model-free methods learn directly from sampled transitions.
    \item Value-based and policy-based methods: value-based methods learn value or action-value functions and choose actions from them. Policy-based methods directly optimize a parameterized policy.
    \item Actor-critic methods: actor-critic methods combine the ideas described above. The actor represents the policy, and the critic estimates values or action-values used to improve that policy.
    \item On-policy and off-policy methods: on-policy methods learn from data generated by the current policy. Off-policy methods can also reuse data generated by previous policies or exploratory behavior.
    \item Tabular and deep RL methods: tabular methods store values explicitly for small state and action spaces. Deep RL uses neural networks as function approximators and is therefore more suitable for large or continuous spaces.
\end{itemize}

The relevant setpoints in this thesis, including battery power, temperature levels, mass flow rates, and bypass positions, are continuous quantities. Discretizing them would either exclude useful operating points or create a very large action space. Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3) and Soft Actor Critic (SAC) are therefore selected because they learn parameterized policies that output continuous actions directly.

Both methods use an actor-critic architecture. The actor maps the current state to continuous setpoint decisions, while the critic estimates their expected long-term effect on operating cost and future system states. TD3 and SAC are applied to learn continuous setpoint policies for both case studies.

\subsubsection{TD3 and SAC}

\paragraph{Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3):}
TD3 uses a deterministic actor, $\mu_\theta(x)$, that maps the current state directly to a continuous action. Two critics estimate the action value, and the smaller estimate is used when constructing the learning target to reduce overestimation bias. TD3 further stabilizes learning through delayed actor updates and target-policy smoothing, in which small noise is added to target actions during critic updates \cite{fujimoto2018addressing}.
\[
J_{\mathrm{TD3}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}
\left[
q_{\phi_1}\bigl(x,\mu_\theta(x)\bigr)
\right].
\]
Here, $J_{\mathrm{TD3}}(\theta)$ is the actor objective; $\theta$ denotes the actor parameters; $\mathcal{D}$ is the replay buffer from which states $x$ are sampled; $\mu_\theta(x)$ is the deterministic action proposed by the actor; and $q_{\phi_1}$ is the first critic, parameterized by $\phi_1$, which estimates the action value. The TD3 actor is trained to maximize this objective, producing one deterministic action, $a=\mu_\theta(x)$, for each state.

\paragraph{Soft Actor-Critic (SAC):}
SAC instead uses a stochastic actor, $\pi_\theta(a\mid x)$, that defines a distribution over continuous actions. In addition to maximizing expected return, SAC includes an entropy term that encourages exploration while training. Like TD3, SAC uses two critics and experience replay; however, its stochastic policy and entropy objective distinguish it from the deterministic TD3 policy \cite{haarnoja2018soft}.
\[
J_{\mathrm{SAC}}(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}^t
\left(
r(x_t,a_t)
+
\alpha\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid x_t))
\right)
\right].
\]
Here, $J_{\mathrm{SAC}}(\pi)$ is the objective of the stochastic policy $\pi$; $\mathbb{E}_{\pi}$ denotes the expectation over trajectories generated by that policy; $N$ is the number of decision steps; $\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}$ is the discount factor; $r(x_t,a_t)$ is the reward at time $t$; $\alpha$ weights the entropy term; and $\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid x_t))$ is the policy entropy.

For a state $x$, the policy entropy is defined as
\[
\mathcal{H}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)
= -\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[\log \pi(a\mid x)\right].
\]
Thus, entropy is an expectation over the policy's action distribution rather than a quantity associated with a single action. During SAC training, an action $a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)$ is sampled from the current policy. The quantity $-\log\pi(a\mid x)$ is a stochastic single-sample estimate of the entropy term: although one sample is generally not equal to the exact entropy, its expectation over actions drawn from the policy equals $\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid x))$. Such stochastic estimates allow SAC to optimize the entropy-regularized objective using minibatch gradient updates \cite{haarnoja2018soft}.

The two algorithms therefore provide a comparison between deterministic and stochastic actor--critic policies for continuous setpoint optimization. Their performance is evaluated using operating cost, constraint satisfaction, and computation time.

\section{Use Cases}
\label{sec:use_case}

This section presents the two case studies used to evaluate reinforcement learning for setpoint determination at different levels of system complexity. The first subsection introduces the battery energy storage system as the simpler case, with one control command and state-of-charge dynamics. The second subsection presents the cookie production factory as the more complex case, with multiple controllable components, nonlinear process behavior, thermal energy storage, and coupled operational constraints.

\subsection{Battery Energy Storage System}

A battery energy storage system (BESS) is an electrochemical device that stores electrical energy for later use and provides grid services such as energy arbitrage, peak shaving, and short-term balancing. A typical BESS is characterized by its usable energy capacity, maximum charge/discharge power, round-trip efficiency, and state-of-charge (SOC) operating limits. Real-world operation must balance economic objectives (e.g., buying electricity at low prices and selling it at high prices) with engineering constraints (SOC bounds, power limits, efficiency losses).

BESS operation presents several challenges. Decisions are inherently time-coupled because current charging and discharging changes future availability. In addition, uncertain exogenous signals (market prices) require policies that are robust to forecast errors, and hard feasibility constraints must be enforced at every time step. These properties make BESS an illustrative case study for comparing optimization-based and reinforcement-learning approaches to real-time setpoint determination in energy systems.

\begin{figure}[!b]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/battery_model.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic of the battery energy storage system. The battery charges from the grid and sells electricity back to the grid; its state of charge $\mathrm{SOC}(t)$ records the stored energy.}
    \label{fig:battery_model}
\end{figure}

A battery energy storage system provides flexible power by buying electricity when prices are low and selling it back to the grid when prices are high. This price-arbitrage operation maximizes profit. A fixed buy--sell price spread $\Delta_s\geq 0$ can be represented by defining the purchase price as $S_t^{\mathrm{buy}}=S_t$ and the selling price as $S_t^{\mathrm{sell}}=S_t-\Delta_s$. In the numerical experiments considered here, $\Delta_s=0$, so the same time-varying electricity price is used for purchases and sales.

The SOC dynamics are modeled as
\[
\mathrm{SOC}_{t+1} = \mathrm{SOC}_t + \frac{P_{\text{actual},t}\,\Delta t}{E_{\max}},
\]
where $P_{\text{actual},t}$ is the effective storage-side power after efficiency losses, $E_{\max}$ is the usable energy capacity, and $\Delta t$ is the time step. The sign convention is $P_t^{\mathrm S}>0$ for charging and grid purchase, and $P_t^{\mathrm S}<0$ for discharging and electricity sale. The superscript $\mathrm S$ identifies power exchanged with the electricity supply grid. The mathematical formulation uses the following smooth power and terminal constraints:

\begin{align}
&P_t^{\mathrm S} = P_{\max}\Big[
\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh\bigl(k(u_t-\delta)\bigr)\right)u_t
\nonumber\\
&\quad {}\times
\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh\bigl(k(0.995-\mathrm{SOC}_t)\bigr)\right)
\nonumber\\
&\quad
+ \frac{1}{2}\left(1-\tanh\bigl(k(u_t+\delta)\bigr)\right)u_t
\nonumber\\
&\quad {}\times
\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh\bigl(k(\mathrm{SOC}_t-0.005)\bigr)\right)
\Big], \quad \forall t, \label{eq:bess_power_mapping} \\
&P_{\text{actual},t} = \frac{P_t^{\mathrm S}}{2}
\Biggl[
\left(1+\tanh\left(\frac{P_t^{\mathrm S}}{\epsilon}\right)\right)\eta
\nonumber\\
&\qquad\quad
+ \left(1-\tanh\left(\frac{P_t^{\mathrm S}}{\epsilon}\right)\right)\frac{1}{\eta}
\Biggr],
\quad \forall t, \label{eq:feasible_actual_power} \\
&\mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{final}} \geq \mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{term}},
\qquad \mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{term}}=0.5.
\label{eq:terminal_soc_requirement}
\end{align}

Here, $u_t$ is the normalized control command and $P_{\max}$ is the maximum charging and discharging power. The parameter $k=700$ controls the steepness of the smooth action and SOC gates: increasing $k$ makes their transitions more abrupt, whereas decreasing it produces a wider and more gradual transition. The parameter $\delta$ shifts the charging and discharging gates away from zero and thereby defines a smooth deadband for small control commands. The implemented value $\delta=0.0025$ corresponds to $0.25\%$ of the maximum normalized action magnitude, or a nominal power command of $0.05\,\mathrm{kW}$ for $P_{\max}=20\,\mathrm{kW}$. This small numerical deadband attenuates near-zero commands and reduces switching around zero while leaving the usable action range effectively unchanged.

The parameter $\eta$ describes the charging and discharging efficiency. The power scale $\epsilon=1\,\mathrm{kW}$ determines the width of the smooth transition between the charging and discharging efficiency relations in Eq.~\eqref{eq:feasible_actual_power}. A larger $\epsilon$ produces a more gradual transition around $P_t^{\mathrm S}=0$, while a smaller value more closely approximates an abrupt change between the two operating directions. Equation~\eqref{eq:bess_power_mapping} maps the normalized action and current SOC to grid-side battery power. It progressively restricts charging as SOC approaches $0.995$ and discharging as SOC approaches $0.005$. Equation~\eqref{eq:feasible_actual_power} maps grid-side power to storage-side power through the smooth efficiency relation.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/battery_smooth_mappings.pdf}
    \caption{Visualization of the smooth battery mappings in Eqs.~(\ref{eq:bess_power_mapping}) and~(\ref{eq:feasible_actual_power}). Panel (a) shows grid-side power as a function of the normalized action for representative SOC values. Panel (b) shows the conversion from grid-side to storage-side power. The plotted parameters are $P_{\max}=20\,\mathrm{kW}$, $k=700$, $\delta=0.0025$, $\eta=0.9$, and $\epsilon=1\,\mathrm{kW}$.}
    \label{fig:battery_smooth_mappings}
\end{figure}

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:battery_smooth_mappings}(a), the mapping is approximately $P_t^{\mathrm S}=P_{\max}u_t$ when the SOC is far from its limits and the action lies outside the narrow deadband. At $\mathrm{SOC}_t=0$, negative actions are suppressed to prevent further discharge; at $\mathrm{SOC}_t=1$, positive actions are suppressed to prevent further charging. At the gate locations $\mathrm{SOC}_t=0.005$ and $\mathrm{SOC}_t=0.995$, the corresponding power is reduced by one half. Figure~\ref{fig:battery_smooth_mappings}(b) illustrates the efficiency relation: away from the transition around zero, positive charging power is multiplied approximately by $\eta$, whereas negative discharging power is multiplied approximately by $1/\eta$. The hyperbolic tangent provides a differentiable transition between these two regimes.

The battery model has a one-dimensional action space at each time step because the scalar command $u_t$ is the only independently selected control variable. Over the complete horizon, the mathematical optimization therefore determines one charging or discharging command for each time step, while battery power and SOC follow from the model equations.

The battery objective is to maximize profit. Let $P_t^+=\max(P_t^{\mathrm S},0)$ denote purchased power and $P_t^-=\max(-P_t^{\mathrm S},0)$ denote sold power. Minimizing the net grid-side cost is equivalent to maximizing profit, giving the general objective
\[
\min_{\{u_t\}_{t=0}^{N-1}} J
= \sum_{t=0}^{N-1}
\left(S_t^{\mathrm{buy}}P_t^+-S_t^{\mathrm{sell}}P_t^-\right)\Delta t.
\]
With $S_t^{\mathrm{buy}}=S_t$ and $S_t^{\mathrm{sell}}=S_t-\Delta_s$, the spread reduces the revenue received for exported electricity. The numerical choice $\Delta_s=0$ yields $S_t^{\mathrm{buy}}=S_t^{\mathrm{sell}}=S_t$ and recovers $J=\sum_t S_tP_t^{\mathrm S}\Delta t$. This equation represents the physical economic objective used to compare the methods. In the mathematical-optimization formulation, the terminal SOC is imposed as the hard constraint in Eq.~\eqref{eq:terminal_soc_requirement}. In the RL formulation, the negative per-step economic contribution is supplemented by a terminal-SOC penalty and, in an additional experimental variant, a progressive terminal-reachability penalty. These penalties guide policy training but are not included in $J$ because they do not represent physical operating costs; their definitions are given in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. The battery purchases electricity from the grid when prices are low and sells it back to the grid when prices are high. The main challenges include respecting SOC and power limits, accounting for efficiency losses, handling uncertain future prices, and managing the fact that actions now affect future energy availability. These features make the BESS a suitable test case for reinforcement learning, where a policy must balance using energy now against saving it for likely higher-price periods while always obeying feasibility limits.

\subsection{Cookie Production Factory}

The second use case represents a nonlinear cookie production factory with an electrified steam-generation utility system. The production process requires a constant supply of process heat in the form of superheated steam, while the utility system must decide how to operate the energy conversion components under fluctuating wind generation $W_t$ and electricity prices $S_t$. The operational objective is to satisfy the steam demand while minimizing grid electricity cost over a finite scheduling horizon. The system model is based on the industrial power-to-heat system introduced by Walden et al.~\cite{walden2023nonlinear} and later used as a renewable steam-generation benchmark by Kyriakidis et al.~\cite{kyriakidis2024hybrid}.

The system consists of four main units: a wind turbine (WT), a closed reverse Brayton-cycle high-temperature heat pump (HTHP), a sensible thermal energy storage (TES), and a steam generator (SG). The WT supplies renewable electricity, while additional electricity can be purchased from the grid. The HTHP uses a waste-heat air stream as its heat source and delivers high-temperature process heat to an intermediate heat-transfer-fluid (HTF) loop. The HTF can be routed through the TES or directly to the SG by controllable bypasses. The SG provides the required steam conditions for the consumer factory. The HTHP and SG are represented by polynomial surrogate models, while the TES is modeled as a simplified single energy reservoir, and the charging/discharging behavior is represented using efficiency equations. Figure~\ref{fig:cookie_steam_system} provides an overview of the complete system, while Fig.~\ref{fig:cookie_detailed_flow_chart} shows the detailed HTF flow paths and bypass variables.

    \caption{Schematic overview of the electrified steam-generation system serving the cookie production factory. Adapted from Fig.~2 of Kyriakidis et al.~\cite{kyriakidis2024hybrid}; the system was originally introduced by Walden et al.~\cite{walden2023nonlinear}.}
    \label{fig:cookie_steam_system}
\end{figure}

    \label{fig:cookie_detailed_flow_chart}
\end{figure}


The corresponding finite-horizon optimization problem is formulated over a discrete time horizon with time index $t \in \{1,\ldots,N\}$:
\begin{align}
&\min_{\{\beta_1^t,\beta_2^t,R^t,\dot m_{\mathrm I}^t,T_{\mathrm I}^t\}_{t=1}^{N}}
\quad J = \sum_{t=1}^{N} P_t^{\mathrm S} S_t \Delta t,
\label{eq:cookie_objective} \\
&\text{s.t.} \quad P_t^{\mathrm S} + W_t = 3F_{\mathrm{HTHP}}\left(T_{\mathrm{I}}^t,\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I}}^t,T_{\mathrm{III}}^t,R^t\right), \label{eq:cookie_power_balance} \\
&T_{\mathrm{II}}^t = F_{\mathrm{HTHX}}\left(T_{\mathrm{I}}^t,\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I}}^t,T_{\mathrm{III}}^t,R^t\right), \\
&T_{\mathrm{IV}}^t = F_{\mathrm{LTHX}}\left(T_{\mathrm{I}}^t,\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I}}^t,T_{\mathrm{III}}^t,R^t\right), \\
&T_2^t = \beta_1^t T_{\mathrm{II}}^t + \left(1-\beta_1^t\right)T_1^t, \\
&T_{\mathrm{I}}^t = \beta_2^t T_3^t + \left(1-\beta_2^t\right)T_4^t, \\
&T_2^t = 201.92 + \frac{1819.32}{3\dot{m}_{\mathrm{II}}^t}, \\
&T_3^t = 196.3 - \frac{188.4}{3\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I}}^t}, \\
&\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{ch}}^t = 3\dot{m}_{\mathrm{II}}^t c_{p,\mathrm{f}}\left(T_{\mathrm{II}}^t-T_1^t\right)\left(1-\beta_1^t\right), \\
&\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{dch}}^t = 3\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I}}^t c_{p,\mathrm{f}}\left(T_4^t-T_3^t\right)\left(1-\beta_2^t\right), \\
&\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{ch}}^t \dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{dch}}^t \leq \gamma, \\
&T_1^t = T_{\mathrm{II}}^t - \epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}\left(T_{\mathrm{II}}^t-T_s^{t-1}\right), \\
&T_4^t = T_3^t - \epsilon_{\mathrm{dch}}\left(T_3^t-T_s^{t-1}\right), \\
&T_s^t = T_s^{t-1} + \frac{\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{ch}}^t-\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{dch}}^t}{m_s c_{p,\mathrm{s}}}\Delta t, \\
&T_s^0 = \tilde{T}_0, \qquad T_s^N = T_s^0, \\
&T_{\mathrm{I}}^t \in [177,250], \qquad \dot{m}_{\mathrm{I}}^t \in [5,16], \\
&T_{\mathrm{III}}^t \in [60,100], \qquad R^t \in [0.8,1.53], \qquad \beta_1^t,\beta_2^t \in [0,1].
\label{eq:cookie_bounds}
\end{align}
In this formulation, the superscript $t$ denotes the value of a quantity at
discrete time step $t$, where $t\in\{1,\ldots,N\}$ and $N$ is the number of
steps in the scheduling horizon. The objective
$J$ is the total grid-electricity cost, $S_t$ is the spot electricity price,
$P_t^{\mathrm S}$ is the purchased grid power, and $W_t$ is the available wind
power. Thus, Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_objective} sums the cost
$P_t^{\mathrm S}S_t\Delta t$ over the horizon. The subscript beneath the
minimum lists the five time-dependent quantities selected independently by the
mathematical optimizer: the two bypass settings $\beta_1^t$ and $\beta_2^t$,
the compressor rotational-speed variable $R^t$, the mass flow
$\dot m_{\mathrm I}^t$, and the HTHP inlet temperature $T_{\mathrm I}^t$.

The Roman subscripts $\mathrm I$--$\mathrm{IV}$ and the Arabic subscripts
$1$--$4$ identify physical locations in the HTF circuit shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:cookie_detailed_flow_chart}; they are not time indices.
$T_{\mathrm I}^t$ and $T_{\mathrm{II}}^t$ are respectively the inlet and outlet
temperatures of the high-temperature heat exchanger (HTHX), whereas
$T_{\mathrm{III}}^t$ and $T_{\mathrm{IV}}^t$ are the inlet and outlet
temperatures of the low-temperature heat exchanger (LTHX). The temperatures
$T_1^t$ and $T_4^t$ are the TES outlet temperatures during charging and
discharging, respectively, while $T_2^t$ and $T_3^t$ are the steam-generator
inlet and outlet temperatures. The quantities $\dot m_{\mathrm I}^t$ and
$\dot m_{\mathrm{II}}^t$ denote the HTHX inlet and outlet HTF mass-flow
rates. The implemented closed-loop model uses the same per-HTHP flow variable
for these two locations. The functions $F_{\mathrm{HTHP}}$,
$F_{\mathrm{HTHX}}$, and $F_{\mathrm{LTHX}}$ are polynomial surrogate models
for the electrical demand of one HTHP, the HTHX outlet temperature, and the
LTHX outlet temperature, respectively.

The bypass settings $\beta_1^t,\beta_2^t\in[0,1]$ determine the TES operating
mode. A value of one fully bypasses the associated storage path: charging uses
$\beta_1^t<1$ and $\beta_2^t=1$, discharging uses $\beta_2^t<1$ and
$\beta_1^t=1$, and $\beta_1^t=\beta_2^t=1$ represents idle operation.
$\dot Q_{s,\mathrm{ch}}^t$ and $\dot Q_{s,\mathrm{dch}}^t$ are the TES charging
and discharging heat-flow rates. The parameter
$c_{p,\mathrm f}$ is the specific heat capacity of the HTF, while
$\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}$ and $\epsilon_{\mathrm{dch}}$ are the charging and
discharging effectiveness factors. The small relaxation parameter $\gamma$
in $\dot Q_{s,\mathrm{ch}}^t\dot Q_{s,\mathrm{dch}}^t\leq\gamma$ prevents
simultaneous charging and discharging when it is chosen close to zero.

Finally, $T_s^t$ is the TES temperature, $m_s$ is the mass of the
storage medium, and $c_{p,\mathrm s}$ is its specific heat capacity. The
quantity $T_s^{t-1}$ is therefore the storage temperature from the preceding
time step. The prescribed initial temperature is $\tilde T_0$, so
$T_s^0=\tilde T_0$, and the equality $T_s^N=T_s^0$ imposes cyclic terminal
operation. The final interval relations specify the validity and operating
bounds of the surrogate model. The power balance in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_power_balance} and the remaining relations through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_bounds} collectively describe the surrogate components,
bypass mixing, TES dynamics, mutually exclusive charging and discharging,
cyclic operation, and admissible operating ranges. All symbols and their units
are also collected in the nomenclature.

% The main control decisions are the bypass variables $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$, the compressor-speed-related variable $R$, the mass flow rate $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I}}$, and the HTHP inlet temperature $T_{\mathrm{I}}$. The bypass variables determine the operating mode of the system. In charging mode, heat from the HTHP is routed into the TES; in discharging mode, stored heat is released to support the steam-generation process; and in idle or bypass operation, the storage is not actively charged or discharged. Simultaneous charging and discharging is prohibited through a complementarity constraint.

% In the reinforcement-learning environment, the agent observes the normalized TES temperature, current spot electricity price $S_t$, available wind power $W_t$, the previous operating mode, and the corresponding look-ahead windows $S_{t+1:t+H}$ and $W_{t+1:t+H}$. The action is a continuous vector containing $\beta_1$, $\beta_2$, $R$, $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I}}$, and $T_{\mathrm{I}}$. The reward is based on the negative grid-electricity cost, with additional penalties for violating operational requirements such as TES temperature bounds, invalid mode behavior, coupling residuals, and deviation from the cyclic terminal storage condition.

The cookie-factory mathematical-optimization model has a five-dimensional action space at each time step. Its independently selected operating variables are the two bypass variables $\beta_1^t$ and $\beta_2^t$, the compressor-speed-related variable $R^t$, the mass flow rate $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I}}^t$, and the HTHP inlet temperature $T_{\mathrm{I}}^t$. The remaining temperatures, power flows, and storage quantities are coupled through the model equations. In the RL formulation, the action space is reduced to two dimensions: the policy selects a signed storage-mode command and the mass flow rate $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I}}^t$. The environment maps the storage-mode command to $\beta_1^t$ and $\beta_2^t$ and derives $R^t$ from the $F_4$ coupling equation. This use case is more complex than the battery system because it combines nonlinear surrogate equations, thermal storage dynamics, and hard process constraints. It therefore provides a suitable second benchmark for evaluating whether reinforcement learning can learn feasible and cost-effective setpoint policies for industrial energy utility systems, and for comparing the learned policy against the existing mathematical optimization formulation.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The previous section introduced the two energy utility systems considered in this thesis and described their operational objectives and constraints. This section explains how these system-specific optimization problems are reformulated as reinforcement-learning problems. In each time step, the agent observes the current system state together with current and forecast external conditions, such as electricity prices and available wind power, selects continuous control actions, and receives a reward based on economic performance and constraint satisfaction. Although the physical models and control variables differ between the two use cases, both are represented using the same agent-environment interaction structure. At each discrete time step, the environment maps the agent's action to physical setpoints, advances the system dynamics, and returns the resulting next state, reward, and termination signals. 

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

For both use cases, the objective is to determine operating setpoints over a discrete time horizon that optimize the relevant economic objective while respecting all constraints. Let $t \in \{0,\ldots,N-1\}$ denote the discrete time index, $S_t$ the spot electricity price, $P_t^{\mathrm S}$ the grid power, and $W_t$ the available wind power. At each time step, the system is described by a state vector $x_t$, the agent selects a continuous action vector $a_t$, and the system evolves to the next state $x_{t+1}$ according to the underlying system dynamics. For the battery case, the only external input is the electricity price $S_t$. For the cookie-factory case, the external inputs are the electricity price $S_t$ and the available wind power $W_t$.

The following subsections specify the state variables, action spaces, transition dynamics, and reward functions for each system.

The reward definitions follow the respective operational objectives and include penalty terms for constraint violations where required. In both cases, feasible and economically favorable operation corresponds to a high cumulative reward. The following subsections describe how this general formulation is implemented in the reinforcement-learning framework, training setup, and evaluation procedure.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning Framework}
\label{sec:rl_framework}

The reinforcement-learning framework used in this thesis follows the same general structure for both use cases. The environments are implemented using Gymnasium \cite{towers2024gymnasium}, while the continuous-control policies are trained using Stable-Baselines3 \cite{raffin2021stablebaselines3}. Each interaction is stored as a transition
\[
\tau_t = \left(x_t,a_t,r_t,x_{t+1},d_t\right),
\]
where $d_t$ indicates whether the episode has ended. In the present environments, reaching the end of the fixed scheduling horizon is represented by the \texttt{terminated} signal. The transitions are stored in a replay buffer and sampled repeatedly during training, enabling the off-policy learning used by SAC and TD3. 
This implementation follows the general agent--environment interaction illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:rl_interaction_loop}, extended by forecast-augmented states, environment-specific action mappings, and constraint handling.

During training, the policy action is first processed by an environment-specific action map
\[
a_t^{\mathrm{phys}} = \mathcal{M}(x_t,a_t),
\]
where $\mathcal{M}$ maps the normalized policy output to physical setpoints and applies environment-specific feasibility handling. Hard constraints are enforced through action bounds, state-dependent mappings, or the system dynamics, while requirements that are not enforced directly are represented by penalty terms in the reward.

For the battery system, the state supplied to the agent consists of the current state of charge, the normalized current spot electricity price $S_t$, the normalized position within the scheduling horizon, and the normalized 24-step price forecast $S_{0:N-1}$. As future work, a rolling-horizon implementation could update the forecast at every decision step. Here, the complete forecast is kept fixed to enable a direct comparison with mathematical optimization over the same single scheduling horizon. The continuous scalar action $u_t\in[-1,1]$ represents the battery charging or discharging command: positive values correspond to charging and negative values to discharging. A smooth, state-dependent action map converts this normalized command into grid-side battery power while progressively restricting charging and discharging near the upper and lower SOC limits.

The reward has the same general form in both environments:
\[
r_t = -C_t - \sum_{\ell} \lambda_\ell v_{\ell,t} - \lambda_{\mathrm{T}} v_{\mathrm{T},t},
\]
where $C_t$ is the grid-electricity cost at time step $t$, $v_{\ell,t}$ are constraint-violation terms, and $\lambda_\ell$ are their corresponding penalty weights. The terminal term $v_{\mathrm{T},t}$ is only active at the end of an episode, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{T}}$ is its penalty weight. In the battery case, it penalizes an insufficient terminal SOC, while in the cookie factory it penalizes deviation from the cyclic terminal TES temperature. This reward design converts the case-specific economic objective into a return-maximization problem without removing the need for physical feasibility.

More specifically, the battery reward implemented in the environment is
\[
r_t = -\frac{S_t P_t^{\mathrm S}\Delta t}{1000}
-\mathbf{1}_{\{t=N-1\}}\lambda_{\mathrm{SOC}}
\max\!\left(\mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{term}}-\mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{final}},0\right),
\]
where $S_t$ is the spot electricity price in EUR/MWh, $P_t^{\mathrm S}$ is the grid-side battery power in kW, and $\Delta t$ is the step duration in hours. This implemented expression is the $\Delta_s=0$ special case of the general buy--sell formulation above. The factor $1000$ converts the energy charge to euros. The terminal term is applied only at the end of an episode and penalizes a final SOC below the prescribed minimum. Intermediate SOC feasibility is handled directly by the battery dynamics and state-dependent power mapping rather than by a constraint-violation penalty.

As an additional feasibility measure, a progressive terminal-SOC reachability penalty was investigated. At each time step, the environment calculates the minimum SOC required to retain a feasible path to the terminal-SOC target under the maximum possible future charging rate:
\[
\mathrm{SOC}^{\mathrm{reach}}_t
= \max\!\left(
\mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{term}}
- (N-t)\frac{\eta P_{\max}\Delta t}{E_{\max}},
0
\right).
\]
Whenever the current SOC falls below this reachability threshold, the shaped reward is
\[
r_t^{\mathrm{reach}}
= r_t
- \lambda_{\mathrm{reach}}
\max\!\left(
\mathrm{SOC}^{\mathrm{reach}}_t-\mathrm{SOC}_t,
0
\right),
\]
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{reach}}>0$ weights the reachability penalty. Unlike the terminal penalty described above, this penalty provides feedback before the final time step and encourages the policy to preserve sufficient energy for the terminal requirement. The resulting feasibility--cost trade-off is reported in Section~\ref{sec:results_discussion}.

For the cookie factory, the observation contains the normalized TES
temperature, current electricity price and wind availability, the previous
operating mode, the previously applied compressor-speed variable and $F_4$
residual, the position within the episode, the terminal-temperature gap, the
previous action, and the complete price and wind forecasts. The policy action
is reduced to
\[
a_t=\left(b_t,\dot m_{\mathrm I}^t\right),
\qquad b_t\in[-1,1],\quad \dot m_{\mathrm I}^t\in[5,16],
\]
where $b_t$ is a signed TES-mode command. The idle band has a half-width of
$\delta_\beta=0.05$. This value was selected as a small numerical tolerance
around zero. It suppresses mode switching caused by small variations in the
policy output while retaining most of the normalized action range for active
charging and discharging. The value was fixed during the experiments and was
not separately optimized. The environment maps $b_t$ to the two physical
bypass settings according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_beta_mapping}:
\begin{equation}
\left(\beta_1^t,\beta_2^t\right)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\left(1,\frac{1+b_t}{1-\delta_\beta}\right),
    & -1\leq b_t<-\delta_\beta \quad \text{(discharging)},\\[2mm]
(1,1),
    & |b_t|\leq\delta_\beta \quad \text{(idle)},\\[1mm]
\displaystyle\left(\frac{1-b_t}{1-\delta_\beta},1\right),
    & \delta_\beta<b_t\leq1 \quad \text{(charging)}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:cookie_beta_mapping}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the inactive path always has a bypass value of one. The mapping
therefore satisfies $\beta_1^t+\beta_2^t\geq1$ and prevents simultaneous TES
charging and discharging without requiring the policy to learn these two
conditions from reward penalties.

After obtaining $\beta_1^t$, $\beta_2^t$, and $\dot m_{\mathrm I}^t$, the
environment evaluates the steam-generator relations, the discharging-side TES
effectiveness relation, and the $F_5$ mixing relation to determine $T_2^t$,
$T_3^t$, $T_4^t$, and $T_{\mathrm I}^t$. The compressor-speed variable $R^t$
is then derived from the $F_4$ coupling relation rather than selected directly
by the policy. Substitution of
$T_1^t=T_{\mathrm{II}}^t-\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}
(T_{\mathrm{II}}^t-T_s^{t-1})$ into $F_4$ gives the required HTHX outlet
temperature in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_required_TII}:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{II,req}}^t=
\frac{T_2^t-\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}(1-\beta_1^t)T_s^{t-1}}
{1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}\beta_1^t}.
\label{eq:cookie_required_TII}
\end{equation}
The environment consequently solves Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_R_derivation}:
\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{HTHX}}\!\left(
T_{\mathrm I}^t,\dot m_{\mathrm I}^t,T_{\mathrm{III}}^t,R^t
\right)-T_{\mathrm{II,req}}^t=0,
\qquad R^t\in[0.8,1.53].
\label{eq:cookie_R_derivation}
\end{equation}
Because the HTHX surrogate is cubic in $R^t$, multiple real roots can occur.
When several bounded roots exist, the root closest to the previously applied
value $R^{t-1}$ is selected to avoid unnecessary switching. At the first time
step, the interval midpoint, $R^0=1.165$, is used as the reference. If no real
root lies within the allowed interval, the bounded value that minimizes the
absolute surrogate mismatch is applied. The remaining mismatch is measured by
the $F_4$ residual
\[
v_{F_4,t}=\left|
T_2^t-\beta_1^tT_{\mathrm{II}}^t
-(1-\beta_1^t)T_1^t
\right|.
\]

\begin{samepage}
The cookie-factory reward contains the economic cost and the constraints that
are not guaranteed by the action mapping. Define $[z]_+=\max(z,0)$,
$\Delta T_s=T_s^{\max}-T_s^{\min}$, and the violation magnitudes
\begin{align*}
v_{s,t}={}&[T_s^{\min}-T_s^{t+1}]_+
          +[T_s^{t+1}-T_s^{\max}]_+,\\
v_{\mathrm I,t}={}&[177-T_{\mathrm I}^t]_+
          +[T_{\mathrm I}^t-250]_+,\\
v_{Q,t}={}&[-\dot Q_{s,\mathrm{ch}}^t]_+
          +[-\dot Q_{s,\mathrm{dch}}^t]_+
          +[\dot Q_{s,\mathrm{ch}}^t-\dot Q_s^{\max}]_+
          +[\dot Q_{s,\mathrm{dch}}^t-\dot Q_s^{\max}]_+,\\
v_{P,t}={}&[-P_t^{\mathrm S}]_+
          +[P_t^{\mathrm S}-P_{\mathrm S}^{\max}]_+.
\end{align*}
\end{samepage}
Here, $v_{s,t}$ measures violations of the TES temperature bounds,
$v_{\mathrm I,t}$ the HTHX inlet-temperature bounds, $v_{Q,t}$ the TES
charging and discharging heat-flow bounds, and $v_{P,t}$ the grid-power bounds.
Each term is zero when its corresponding constraint is satisfied.
For the one-hour time steps used here, the grid cost is
$C_t=S_tP_t^{\mathrm S}/1000$. The implemented reward is
\begin{align}
r_t^{\mathrm{cookie}}={}&-C_t
-\lambda_s\frac{v_{s,t}}{\Delta T_s}
-\lambda_{\mathrm I}\frac{v_{\mathrm I,t}}{250-177}
-\lambda_Q\frac{v_{Q,t}}{\dot Q_s^{\max}}
-\lambda_{F_4}\frac{v_{F_4,t}}{\Delta T_s}
\nonumber\\
&-\lambda_P\frac{v_{P,t}}{P_{\mathrm S}^{\max}}
-\mathbf{1}_{\{t=N-1\}}\lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}}
 \frac{|T_s^N-T_s^0|}{\Delta T_s}.
\label{eq:cookie_reward}
\end{align}
The values used in training are $T_s^{\min}=183\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$,
$T_s^{\max}=324\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$,
$\dot Q_s^{\max}=5000\,\mathrm{kW}$,
$P_{\mathrm S}^{\max}=5000\,\mathrm{kW}$,
$\lambda_s=20$, $\lambda_{\mathrm I}=10$, $\lambda_Q=5000$,
$\lambda_{F_4}=500$, $\lambda_P=3000$, and
$\lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}}=3000$. These coefficients were selected empirically
during training. Because each violation is normalized
before weighting, the coefficient magnitudes express relative priorities rather
than compensating for different physical units. The comparatively large
$\lambda_Q$ and $\lambda_P$ strongly discourage heat-flow and grid-power
violations. The terminal coefficient $\lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}}$ is also large
because its penalty is applied only once, at the end of the episode. The smaller $\lambda_s$ and
$\lambda_{\mathrm I}$ reflect that the storage-state and inlet-temperature
bounds were largely satisfied during preliminary runs. These values are fixed
engineering choices rather than proven optimal coefficients. Thus, the physical
operating cost remains identifiable within the reward, while the normalized
penalties provide training feedback for state, inlet-temperature, heat-flow,
$F_4$-coupling, grid-power, and terminal-cyclic violations.

After training, both policies are evaluated deterministically to compare schedules. For SAC, the mean action of the stochastic policy is selected at each time step. TD3 directly outputs a deterministic action. This produces one reproducible setpoint trajectory for a given price and wind profile and allows direct comparison with mathematical optimization in terms of cost, constraint satisfaction, and computation time.

\subsection{Training Setup}


Training is performed in reinforcement-learning environments before the learned policies are evaluated on fixed test trajectories. The environment provides the transition dynamics, cost calculation, constraint handling, and terminal condition for each episode. The learning algorithm only receives observations, rewards, and termination signals through the environment interface.

For both use cases, one episode corresponds to a 24-step scheduling horizon. During training, the start index of the episode is sampled randomly from the available time-series data. This random-window strategy exposes the agent to different price and renewable-generation patterns instead of repeatedly starting each episode at the same time of day. Both environments use the grid-price series; the cookie factory environment additionally samples the corresponding wind-availability values for the same 24-hour window.

The training environments are wrapped with monitoring and vectorized environment interfaces. Monitoring records episode rewards and lengths for later inspection, while the vectorized wrapper provides the interface expected by the Stable-Baselines3 implementation of SAC and TD3. Only one environment instance is used at a time in the current setup, so the vectorization is mainly an interface layer rather than a parallel simulation strategy.

The environment-specific action mappings and reward definitions are given in
Section~\ref{sec:rl_framework}, including the cookie-factory reward in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_reward}. The cookie-factory penalties are particularly important because
its coupled nonlinear feasibility requirements cannot all be represented by
simple action bounds.

Hyperparameter selection included 50 tuning trials. The final training settings, which are shared by the battery and cookie-factory case studies, are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:sac_training_setup}. The environments themselves still differ in their state variables, action spaces, dynamics, and constraint structures.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

The trained RL policies are evaluated through deterministic rollouts on fixed 24-hour evaluation windows. This makes the resulting setpoint trajectory reproducible and allows a direct comparison with mathematical optimization on the same grid-price and wind-availability profiles.

The primary economic metric is the total net grid-side cost over the evaluation horizon:
\[
J_{\mathrm{grid}} = \sum_{t=0}^{N-1} C_t,
\]
where $C_t$ is the grid-electricity cost at time step $t$. For the battery system, this cost may generally include the fixed buy--sell spread $\Delta_s$ defined above; the numerical experiments use $\Delta_s=0$. Minimizing $J_{\mathrm{grid}}$ is equivalent to maximizing profit. For the cookie factory, the cost is computed from the grid power required by the heat-pump system after accounting for available wind power and is minimized directly. Thus, a lower value of $J_{\mathrm{grid}}$ indicates higher profit for the battery and lower operating cost for the cookie factory.

The cumulative reward is reported as a secondary metric:
\[
R_{\mathrm{ep}} = \sum_{t=0}^{N-1} r_t.
\]
This value is useful for analyzing the RL objective because it combines the economic term with penalty terms. However, it is not used as the only comparison criterion, since the reward may include artificial penalty weights that are not part of the physical economic objective. Therefore, the economic objective and constraint satisfaction are evaluated separately.

Constraint satisfaction is measured by aggregating the violation terms recorded during the rollout. For a set of constraint residuals or violation magnitudes $v_{\ell,t}$, the total violation is defined as
\[
V_{\mathrm{tot}} = \sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\sum_{\ell} v_{\ell,t}.
\]
In addition, the number or frequency of time steps with nonzero violations is considered:
\[
f_{\mathrm{viol}} =
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}
\mathbb{I}\left(\sum_{\ell} v_{\ell,t} > \varepsilon\right),
\]
where $\varepsilon$ is a small numerical tolerance. For the battery system, the relevant checks include SOC bounds, power limits, and the terminal SOC requirement. For the cookie factory, the violation categories include bypass feasibility, complementarity of charging and discharging, TES temperature bounds, grid-power bounds, coupling residuals, and the terminal cyclic TES condition.

The evaluation also records trajectory-level operating indicators. For the BESS, these include the SOC trajectory, commanded and effective battery power, and terminal SOC.

Finally, computation time is used to assess practical applicability. For mathematical optimization, the relevant value is the solver time required to compute a complete schedule for the evaluation horizon. For RL, the one-time training effort is reported separately from the online evaluation time. This distinction is important because RL can require substantial training effort, but a trained policy can usually produce setpoints much faster than solving a nonlinear optimization problem from scratch for every horizon.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results_discussion}


The previous section described the RL formulation, training setup, and evaluation procedure for both use cases. This section presents and discusses the resulting policy performance relative to mathematical optimization. All computations were performed in Python~3.14.1, with Gymnasium~1.2.2 used to implement the reinforcement-learning environments and Stable-Baselines3~2.7.0, based on PyTorch~2.9.1, used to train the RL policies. Experiments were conducted on an AMD Ryzen~7 7840HS CPU with Radeon~780M Graphics. Offline training time is excluded from all online computation time comparisons reported here.

A preliminary learning-rate study was conducted for SAC using the battery environment. A constant learning rate, a linear-decay schedule, and a power-law-decay schedule were each trained and evaluated over 32 independent runs using the same evaluation setup. The linear schedule achieved the highest mean cumulative reward, $8.9962 \pm 0.0286$, and the lowest mean objective value, $-9.00 \pm 0.03$~EUR. The corresponding mean rewards were $8.9891 \pm 0.0325$ for the constant schedule and $8.9809 \pm 0.0439$ for the power-law schedule.

The differences between the schedules were small relative to the variation across runs, indicating that the battery environment was not highly sensitive to the exact learning-rate decay shape in this experiment. The linear schedule was therefore selected for the subsequent SAC and TD3 training in both case studies and for the comparisons with mathematical optimization.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion_future_work}

\subsection{Conclusion}

This thesis investigated whether reinforcement learning can determine feasible and cost-effective operating setpoints for energy utility systems of different complexity. The setpoint-selection tasks were formulated as finite-horizon Markov decision processes and evaluated for a battery energy storage system and the electrified steam-generation utility system of a cookie production factory. The battery represents a one-dimensional storage-control problem, whereas the cookie-factory mathematical-optimization model contains five coupled control variables and nonlinear process constraints. For the cookie-factory RL formulation, the action space was reduced to two dimensions by allowing the policy to select a signed storage-mode command and mass flow while deriving the remaining quantities through the process equations. In both cases, the trained policies were compared with mathematical optimization using the original economic objective, constraint satisfaction, and online computation time.

For the battery system, mathematical optimization achieved the highest mean profit, but the TD3 policy approached its performance closely. Across 1000 randomly sampled 24-hour windows, the mean objective was $-3.02$~EUR for TD3 and $-3.19$~EUR for mathematical optimization, giving a mean gap of 0.16~EUR. TD3 also produced SOC trajectories that used a similar portion of the available storage range. Its main advantage was speed: a complete policy rollout required 0.0082~s on average, compared with 0.5605~s for mathematical optimization, corresponding to a speedup of approximately $68.13\times$.

The cookie factory presented a more difficult control problem because nonlinear surrogate equations, storage dynamics, and process constraints must be satisfied simultaneously. Across the 200 matched comparisons with mathematical optimization, SAC produced a mean cost gap of 80.33~EUR and a mean absolute relative gap of 6.31\%, while TD3 produced a smaller mean gap of 69.11~EUR and a smaller mean absolute relative gap of 5.66\%. SAC and TD3 generated schedules in 0.0217~s and 0.0180~s on average, corresponding to speedups of approximately $533.81\times$ and $645.06\times$, respectively. TD3 is therefore selected as the better cookie-factory RL policy overall based on its economic performance, online speed, and smaller grid-power violation. However, SAC yielded smaller $F_4$ residuals, heat-flow-bound violations, and terminal cyclic deviations. Both policies remained substantially less feasible than mathematical optimization.

These online speed advantages required offline training. Training the battery policies required 36 minutes for SAC and 23 minutes for TD3, while training the cookie-factory policies required 2.81 hours and 1.24 hours, respectively.

The results therefore provide a qualified answer to the research question. Reinforcement learning can learn continuous setpoint policies that produce economically meaningful schedules with very low online computation time. Its practical value is strongest when decisions must be generated repeatedly under short computation time limits and when offline training can be performed in advance. In the simpler battery system, the learned policy remained close to mathematical optimization. As system complexity and constraint coupling increased, the gap in operating cost and feasibility became more pronounced. Mathematical optimization remained superior when the best objective value and reliable constraint satisfaction were the primary requirements.

\subsection{Future Work}

Constraint handling should be strengthened for the cookie-factory policy. In particular, the observed grid-power violations should be addressed through a constraint-preserving action mapping or action projection. The two-dimensional action formulation should also be investigated further to determine how the derived compressor-speed calculation and storage-mode mapping affect feasibility, sensitivity, and learning stability.

The present evaluation uses fixed 24-hour price and wind profiles that are available to both methods. A rolling-horizon implementation should instead update forecasts and setpoints as new measurements become available. This would allow the policies to be tested under forecast errors, measurement noise, disturbances, and model--plant mismatch. Domain randomization or targeted retraining could then be used to improve robustness to operating conditions not represented in the original training data.

The mathematical-optimization comparison can also be extended. The schedule generated by the RL policy could be used to warm-start IPOPT, while hybrid methods such as BO-IPOPT should be evaluated on the same windows and computation budgets. Such hybrid approaches could combine rapid RL setpoint generation with mathematical optimization to restore feasibility and improve the objective. Finally, the proposed policies should be validated using a higher-fidelity real-plant implementation.
