\newcommand{\ABSTRACT}{
Energy utility systems operate under dynamic conditions in which electricity
prices, renewable generation, storage states, and interacting process
variables affect operating cost, efficiency, reliability, and constraint
satisfaction. Improving their operation can also support renewable-energy
integration and reduce energy-related carbon emissions. Their setpoints are
conventionally determined by solving constrained finite-horizon optimization problems, which depend on
accurate system models and can become computationally demanding as system
complexity increases. Time-varying inputs, renewable-energy uncertainty, and
model--plant mismatch further motivate the investigation of data-driven
alternatives. This thesis therefore evaluates whether reinforcement learning
can provide effective setpoints with low online computation time for two
systems of different complexity: a battery energy storage system and the
electrified steam-generation utility system of a cookie production factory.

Both problems are formulated as finite-horizon Markov decision processes, and actor-critic agents are trained using Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) and Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3). IPOPT is used to solve the finite-horizon nonlinear programs and serves as the optimization baseline for both systems. Under identical input data, the schedules produced by the RL policies are compared with this baseline in terms of economic objective, feasibility, and online computation time.

For the battery system, TD3 closely approached the economic performance of
the optimization baseline while generating schedules approximately
$68\times$ faster. For the more complex cookie factory, SAC and TD3 achieved
mean absolute relative objective gaps of 6.31\% and 5.66\%, respectively,
while generating setpoints approximately $534\times$ and $645\times$ faster
than the optimization baseline. Although SAC had the larger economic gap, it
produced smaller $F_4$-coupling residuals, heat-flow-bound violations, and
terminal cyclic deviations, whereas TD3 produced smaller grid-power
violations. Both cookie-factory policies produced constraint violations,
whereas the optimization baseline remained feasible.

The results show that trained reinforcement-learning policies can generate
economically meaningful setpoints with very low online computation time after
offline training, although some constraint violations remained in the more
complex cookie-factory model.

\section{Introduction}
\label{Section1}

Modern energy utility systems supply the electricity and heat needed to sustain industrial production and other essential activities. Their efficient operation is important for meeting energy demand while limiting operating costs and environmental impacts. Key objectives of industrial energy management include reducing operating costs and CO$_2$ emissions, improving energy efficiency, and integrating renewable energy sources. Achieving these objectives requires coordinated decisions about energy conversion, storage, and supply while satisfying the requirements of the processes being served.

These operating decisions are shaped by dynamic conditions and multiple sources of uncertainty. Electricity prices vary over time, renewable generation is weather dependent, and storage systems couple current decisions with future operating flexibility \cite{khalid2024smart}. At the same time, industrial energy systems often exhibit nonlinear component behavior and include operating and coupling constraints that must be respected at every time step. The resulting setpoint selection problem is therefore not only an economic problem, but also a constrained sequential decision-making problem.

In many engineering applications, such setpoints are determined by solving constrained finite-horizon optimization problems. This approach is attractive because physical constraints and objective functions can be formulated explicitly, and mature solvers can compute high-quality solutions for well-defined models. For energy systems, nonlinear programming methods such as IPOPT \cite{wachter2006implementation} are commonly used when component models, storage dynamics, and operational constraints are nonlinear \cite{walden2023nonlinear}. However, deterministic global optimization can become computationally demanding for large-scale or nonconvex problems, making it difficult to obtain a globally optimal solution within a practical time limit \cite{lin2012deterministic}. Stochastic global optimization methods can also require many objective-function evaluations and exhibit slow convergence, with computational cost increasing as the problem dimension grows \cite{zhigljavsky2008stochastic}. In industrial energy management, forecast uncertainty, optimization-horizon length, and computation time per rolling-horizon iteration are additional factors that influence system performance \cite{kyriakidis2025energy}.

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a different perspective. Unlike classical control and optimization methods that solve a trajectory-optimization problem from scratch at each operating horizon, RL agents learn a policy or action-value function gradually through interactive experience. Once trained, this learned representation enables rapid selection of actions from the current observation \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. This makes RL attractive for real-time or repeated decision-making problems, especially when the system is complex, nonlinear, and operates in a changing environment \cite{pilling2025rl}. Nevertheless, RL does not automatically guarantee feasibility or optimality. For engineering use, a learned policy must be evaluated not only by its reward, but also by its operating cost, constraint satisfaction, and computation time relative to established optimization methods.

This thesis investigates whether RL can be used for optimal setpoint determination in energy utility systems of different complexity. Two case studies are considered to examine how the number of control variables, nonlinear component behavior, and coupling between operational constraints affect the applicability of RL. The first is a battery energy storage system (BESS), which represents a comparatively simple storage-control problem with a single charging or discharging command, state-of-charge dynamics, and time-varying electricity prices. The second is the electrified steam-generation utility system of a cookie production factory, incorporating wind power and thermal energy storage. Based on the benchmark systems described by Walden et al. and Kyriakidis et al. \cite{walden2023nonlinear,kyriakidis2024hybrid}, this case has greater complexity because it combines multiple continuous control variables, nonlinear heat-pump and heat-transfer behavior, and coupled mass-flow, temperature, and storage constraints. Comparing the two cases allows the benefits and limitations of RL to be assessed across a simpler storage system and a more complex industrial energy utility system.

The central research question of this thesis is:
\begin{quote}
\itshape Can reinforcement learning determine feasible and cost-effective operating setpoints for energy utility systems of varying complexity?
\end{quote}
This question is addressed through the following subquestions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item How can the setpoint optimization problem be formulated as a Markov decision process with suitable states, actions, and rewards?
    \item Can deep reinforcement-learning policies learn continuous setpoint decisions that respect the system dynamics and operational constraints of the two systems?
    \item How does the learned policy compare with the optimization baseline in terms of operating cost, constraint satisfaction, and computation time?
    \item Under which conditions does RL offer a practical advantage, and where do classical optimization methods remain superior?
\end{itemize}

For each case study, the setpoint optimization problem is first formulated as a Markov decision process (MDP). Deep neural networks are used to parameterize the RL policies that map system observations to continuous setpoint decisions. The resulting policies are evaluated through deterministic rollouts in reinforcement-learning environments, and the resulting schedules are compared with the optimization baseline. The comparison is based on total grid-electricity cost, constraint violations, and computation time. This separation is important because a policy with high cumulative reward is only useful if it remains feasible and economically meaningful under the original engineering objective.

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows: Section~\ref{sec:theory_background} establishes the theoretical foundations of setpoint optimization and reinforcement learning, introduces IPOPT as the optimization baseline, and describes the TD3 and SAC algorithms considered in this work. Section~\ref{sec:battery_case} presents the battery energy storage system, followed by its problem formulation, methodology, and numerical results. The implementation, training procedure, and evaluation metrics shared by both case studies are introduced within the battery methodology. Section~\ref{sec:cookie_case} then presents the cookie production factory in the same order: problem formulation, methodology, and numerical results. Each case study compares the learned policies with the optimization baseline in terms of economic performance, constraint satisfaction, and computation time. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusion_future_work} summarizes the main findings, identifies the limitations of the study, and outlines directions for future work.

\section{Theory and Background}
\label{sec:theory_background}

This section provides the theoretical basis for the setpoint optimization problem studied in this thesis. The central question is how operating setpoints in energy utility systems can be selected over time so that costs are reduced while physical and operational limits are respected. The same problem is studied through two computational approaches: direct solution of a finite-horizon constrained nonlinear program for each input window and action selection by RL policies trained offline. The section first introduces the model-based optimization formulation and its solution with IPOPT. It then presents the fundamental concepts of RL and the MDP formulation \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}, followed by descriptions of the TD3 \cite{fujimoto2018addressing} and SAC \cite{haarnoja2018soft} algorithms for continuous control evaluated in this work.

Here, RL is not treated as a substitute for physical constraints. Instead, the setpoint optimization problem is formulated as an MDP, with feasibility enforced where possible through action bounds, state-dependent mappings, and system dynamics, and otherwise discouraged through reward penalties. This distinction is important when comparing RL with the optimization baseline, because a low-cost policy is only useful if its decisions remain feasible.

\subsection{Setpoint Optimization in Energy Utility Systems}

Energy utility systems are operated by choosing setpoints that determine how components such as storage units, heat pumps, valves, and bypasses are controlled. A setpoint describes the desired value of a control variable. In the systems considered here, this may be the charging or discharging power of a battery, the bypass variable $\beta_{1,t}$, or a mass flow rate. These choices are not just technical details; they directly affect operating cost, energy efficiency, constraint satisfaction, and overall system reliability and lifetime \cite{tran2025safe}.

Setpoint determination is also inherently sequential. The best operating point at one time step cannot be chosen independently of what happened before or what may be needed later. Storage systems make this especially clear. Charging a battery or a thermal energy storage system can increase future flexibility, but it may also increase the current electricity cost. Discharging can reduce current grid consumption or generate sale revenue, but it leaves less stored energy for later. Good setpoint decisions must therefore account for inter-temporal dependencies and physical operating limits \cite{walden2023nonlinear}. Each action affects both the immediate economic outcome and the feasible decisions available at later time steps; consequently, its quality must be evaluated over the full planning horizon rather than solely at the current time step \cite{takam2025stochastic}.

In practical operation, the controller also has to react to external signals such as electricity prices, renewable generation, production demand, and ambient conditions. Since these signals change over time, fixed rule-based strategies can become inefficient, and the operation can be formulated as a finite-horizon decision problem with time-varying boundary conditions \cite{meng2025online,pilling2025rl}. This motivates two solution approaches for the same setpoint determination task: solving a constrained finite-horizon problem directly and applying a policy learned through reinforcement learning.

\subsection{Model-Based Optimization}

Model-based optimization provides a natural way to describe setpoint selection. In general, optimization means selecting the best decision from a set of feasible alternatives according to a defined objective. For energy systems, the objective is often to minimize operating costs, energy consumption, or CO$_2$ emissions, while the feasible set is determined by physical equations and operational limits \cite{walden2023nonlinear,kyriakidis2025energy}. A constrained optimization problem can be written as \cite{nocedal2006numerical}:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\xi \in \Omega} f(\xi) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad h(\xi) = 0,\; c(\xi) \le 0,
\label{eq:general_constrained_optimization}
\end{equation}
Here, $\xi \in \Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n_\xi}$ denotes the decision vector, $f : \mathbb{R}^{n_\xi} \to \mathbb{R}$ is the objective function, $h : \mathbb{R}^{n_\xi} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_h}$ represents the equality constraints, and $c : \mathbb{R}^{n_\xi} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_c}$ represents the inequality constraints. The solution $\xi^\star$ is optimal if it minimizes the objective while satisfying all constraints.

For finite-horizon setpoint optimization, the decision vector contains the control actions for several time steps. The objective is usually the accumulated operating cost over the horizon. The constraints describe system dynamics, energy balances, storage bounds, and terminal requirements. This time-coupled structure is important because a state such as battery state of charge or thermal storage temperature depends on previous decisions.

The difficulty of an optimization problem depends strongly on its mathematical structure. Linear and mixed-integer linear formulations are widely used in energy-system applications because simplified models can often be solved reliably. However, realistic energy conversion systems frequently include nonlinear component behavior, continuous control variables, and coupled dynamic constraints. Such systems lead to nonlinear programming (NLP) formulations, which are generally harder to solve and may contain local optima \cite{nocedal2006numerical}. As the number of time steps, decision variables, nonlinear component models, and coupling constraints increases, the optimization problem generally becomes larger and more difficult to solve within a short operational time window \cite{kyriakidis2024hybrid}.

Another important distinction is whether the problem is deterministic or stochastic. In deterministic optimization, all relevant parameters, such as electricity prices, wind availability, and demand, are assumed to be known over the optimization horizon. Stochastic optimization represents uncertain quantities through random variables or scenarios. This is closer to real operation, but it also increases model size and computational complexity.

Nonlinear constrained problems are usually solved with dedicated numerical solvers. Interior-point methods are common in this setting. Wächter and Biegler introduce IPOPT as an open-source solver for large-scale nonlinear programming that uses a primal-dual interior-point method with a filter line-search strategy \cite{wachter2006implementation}.

More advanced hybrid optimization methods, such as Bayesian Optimization-Interior Point OPTimizer (BO-IPOPT), have been developed for complex nonlinear, nonconvex, and constrained optimization problems. BO-IPOPT combines the global exploration capability of Bayesian optimization with the efficient local convergence and constraint handling of IPOPT. The method has been evaluated for the optimal operation of an industrial energy conversion system and subsequently enhanced for constrained problems of varying dimensionality, multimodality, and nonlinearity \cite{kyriakidis2024hybrid,kyriakidis2025enhanced}. In this thesis, IPOPT is used to solve the finite-horizon nonlinear programs and serves as the optimization baseline. The evaluation of BO-IPOPT as an additional global optimization method is left for future work.

IPOPT handles the inequality constraints in Eq.~\eqref{eq:general_constrained_optimization} by introducing the slack vector $s=(s_1,\ldots,s_{n_c})^\top$. Each inequality $c_j(\xi)\leq 0$ is then expressed as $c_j(\xi)+s_j=0$ with $s_j\geq 0$. To keep the slack variables strictly positive during the optimization, the interior-point formulation augments the objective with a logarithmic barrier parameterized by $\mu_{\mathrm b}>0$. This yields the barrier subproblem \cite{wachter2006implementation}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\xi,s}\quad & f(\xi) - \mu_{\mathrm b}\sum_{j=1}^{n_c}\ln(s_j) \\
\text{s.t.}\quad & h(\xi)=0, \qquad c(\xi)+s=0, \qquad s>0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ipopt_barrier_problem}
\end{equation}
The logarithmic barrier prevents the iterates from reaching the constraint boundary. IPOPT solves a sequence of such subproblems using a primal-dual Newton method. With equality multipliers $y$ and inequality multipliers $z$, the perturbed Karush-Kuhn-Tucker(KKT) conditions are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\nabla f(\xi)+J_h(\xi)^\top y+J_c(\xi)^\top z &= 0, \\
h(\xi) &= 0, \qquad c(\xi)+s=0, \\
\mathsf{S} z &= \mu_{\mathrm b}\mathbf{1}, \qquad s>0,\; z>0,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ipopt_kkt}
\end{equation}
where $J_h$ and $J_c$ are the Jacobians of the equality and inequality constraints, respectively, and $\mathsf{S}=\operatorname{diag}(s)$. As $\mu_{\mathrm b}$ is reduced towards zero, the perturbed complementarity condition $\mathsf{S}z=\mu_{\mathrm b}\mathbf{1}$ approaches the KKT complementarity condition of the original constrained problem \cite{wachter2006implementation}.

Nocedal and Wright note that the performance of nonlinear optimization algorithms depends on formulation quality, particularly the constraint structure, variable scaling, and initial point \cite{nocedal2006numerical}.

The KKT conditions are not specific to IPOPT. They are also relevant when an RL-generated schedule is evaluated as a solution to the original constrained problem. To be described as locally optimal, the RL schedule would need to satisfy the original constraints and the KKT conditions.

The RL methods used in this thesis do not explicitly enforce the KKT conditions. Therefore, the RL schedules are not claimed to be optimal. Their objective values and constraint violations are reported separately and compared with the IPOPT optimization baseline. The main motivation for investigating RL is its potential to generate control decisions quickly once training has been completed offline.

The following section introduces the fundamental principles of RL and its formulation for setpoint optimization.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning}

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a different view of the same setpoint problem. Instead of solving an optimization problem from scratch at every decision time, an agent learns from repeated interaction with an environment. At each time step, the agent observes the current state, selects an action, receives a reward, and observes the next state. Over time, it adapts its policy so that actions leading to high long-term reward become more likely \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.

\begin{figure}[!tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/markov_diagram_v2.pdf}
    \caption{Agent--environment interaction using the notation of this thesis. At time $t$, the agent observes state $x_t$ and selects $a_t\in D_t(x_t)$; the environment evaluates $r_t(x_t,a_t)$ and generates $x_{t+1}$ according to $Q_t(\cdot\mid x_t,a_t)$.}
    \label{fig:rl_interaction_loop}
\end{figure}

Following Bäuerle and Rieder \cite{baeuerle2011markov}, a non-stationary Markov decision model with planning horizon $N\in\mathbb{N}$ consists of the data
\[
\left(E,A,D_t,Q_t,r_t,g_N\right),
\]
with the following meaning for $t=0,1,\ldots,N-1$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $E$ is the state space and $A$ is the action space,
    \item $D_t(x)\subseteq A$ is the set of admissible actions in state $x$ at time $t$,
    \item $Q_t(\cdot\mid x,a)$ is the transition kernel,
    \item $r_t(x,a)$ is the stage-reward function, and
    \item $g_N(x)$ is the terminal-reward function.
\end{itemize}

Under the Markov property, the current state contains all information required to predict the next state, given the selected action. A non-stationary policy is a sequence $\pi=(\pi_t)_{t=0}^{N-1}$, where $\pi_t(\cdot\mid x)$ is the conditional action distribution in state $x$ at time $t$. Thus, $\pi_t$ defines the action-selection rule at time $t$, conditional on the current state $x$. For a deterministic policy, this distribution assigns all probability to one action. Following the finite-horizon convention of Bäuerle and Rieder \cite{baeuerle2011markov}, the total return from time $t$ is
\begin{samepage}
\[
\begin{aligned}
G_t & = \sum_{i=t}^{N-1}\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}^{\,i-t}r_i(X_i,A_i)
      +\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}^{\,N-t}g_N(X_N),\\
\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}} & = 1 \quad \text{for the finite-horizon problems considered here}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Because $\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}=1$ in all experiments, all discount powers equal unity, and the expression reduces to the undiscounted total-reward form used by Bäuerle and Rieder.
\end{samepage}
The terminal reward $g_N(X_N)$ is evaluated at the terminal state. In the implemented environments, $g_N\equiv0$ because terminal penalties are included in the stage reward returned with the final transition.

\paragraph{Bellman equations and RL solution approaches.}

The learning objective is to find a policy that maximizes the expected return. The value function and action-value function of a policy $\pi$ are defined as
\[
v_t^\pi(x) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[G_t \mid X_t=x\right],
\qquad
q_t^\pi(x,a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[G_t \mid X_t=x, A_t=a\right].
\]
The value function evaluates a state before an action is selected, whereas the action-value function evaluates a particular state--action pair. For a fixed policy, they satisfy the Bellman expectation equations
\begin{align}
q_t^\pi(x,a)
&=r_t(x,a)+\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}
  \int_E v_{t+1}^\pi(x')\,Q_t(\mathrm{d}x'\mid x,a),
  \label{eq:bellman_action_value}\\
v_t^\pi(x)
&=\int_{D_t(x)}q_t^\pi(x,a)\,\pi_t(\mathrm{d}a\mid x),
  \qquad t=0,\ldots,N-1,
  \label{eq:bellman_state_value}
\end{align}
Here, $x'$ is a dummy variable representing a possible next state. If densities exist, these measures may be written as $Q_t(\mathrm{d}x'\mid x,a)=p_t(x'\mid x,a)\,\mathrm{d}x'$ and $\pi_t(\mathrm{d}a\mid x)=\pi_t(a\mid x)\,\mathrm{d}a$; for discrete state or action spaces, the corresponding integrals become sums.

With terminal condition $v_N^\pi(x)=g_N(x)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:bellman_action_value} separates the value of an action into its immediate reward and its expected discounted future value. Equation~\eqref{eq:bellman_state_value} averages the action values according to the policy. For a deterministic policy, this average reduces to the value of the single selected action.

The Bellman optimality equations describe the best attainable value rather than the value under a fixed policy:
\[
\begin{aligned}
q_t^\star(x,a)
&=r_t(x,a)+\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}
  \int_E v_{t+1}^\star(x')\,Q_t(\mathrm{d}x'\mid x,a),\\
v_t^\star(x)
&=\sup_{a\in D_t(x)}q_t^\star(x,a),
\end{aligned}
\qquad t=0,\ldots,N-1,
\]
with terminal condition $v_N^\star(x)=g_N(x)$. Thus, the Bellman expectation equations evaluate a specified policy, while the Bellman optimality equations select the action with the greatest value. Both express the same recursive relationship: current value equals immediate reward plus the value of subsequent decisions \cite{baeuerle2011markov,sutton2018reinforcement}.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning and temporal-difference learning.}
Model-free RL learns a decision rule from sampled transitions without requiring the transition kernel $Q_t$ in explicit form. A transition $(x_t,a_t,r_t,x_{t+1},d_t)$ contains the current state and action, the resulting reward and successor state, and a terminal indicator $d_t$. Here and in the learning updates below, $r_t$ is shorthand for the realized stage reward $r_t(x_t,a_t)$. Although the samples in this thesis are generated by simulation, the learning algorithms update their neural networks from these transition tuples rather than from the simulator equations. The implemented state includes the position within the scheduling horizon, so the neural-network policies and critics do not require a separate time subscript.

Temporal-difference (TD) learning updates a value approximation using the observed reward and an estimate of the value at the next state. For an action-value approximation $q_\phi$ with parameter vector $\phi$, a generic one-step TD target is
\[
y_t=r_t+\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}(1-d_t)\widehat v^{\mathrm{tar}}(x_{t+1}),
\]
and the corresponding TD error is
\[
\delta_t^{\mathrm{TD}}=y_t-q_\phi(x_t,a_t).
\]
The target combines the observed immediate reward with an estimate of the remaining return. The notation $\widehat v^{\mathrm{tar}}$ denotes this estimate at $x_{t+1}$. The factor $(1-d_t)$ removes the continuation value at a terminal transition. The superscript $\mathrm{tar}$ indicates that the continuation estimate is treated as fixed during the gradient step and is typically constructed using slowly updated target networks. The action-value approximation is trained by minimizing the mean squared TD error. This update builds directly on the Bellman relationship while learning from sampled transitions \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.

\paragraph{Policy gradients and actor--critic learning.}
A critic estimates the long-term quality of actions, but a continuous control problem also requires an efficient way to select an action. Directly maximizing $q_\phi(x,a)$ over every possible continuous action at every decision step can be costly. Policy-gradient methods instead represent the policy by an actor with parameters $\theta$ and adjust those parameters in the direction of higher expected return.

For a stochastic policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid x)$, the policy-gradient principle weights the score $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid x)$ by an estimate of how advantageous the sampled action was:
\[
\nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{PG}}(\theta)
\approx
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid x)\,\mathcal{A}_t^\pi(x,a)
\right],
\qquad
\mathcal{A}_t^\pi(x,a)=q_t^\pi(x,a)-v_t^\pi(x).
\]
Here, $J_{\mathrm{PG}}(\theta)$ is the generic policy-gradient objective and $\mathcal{A}_t^\pi$ is the advantage function; $A$ remains the action space. The baseline $v_t^\pi(x)$ does not change the expected gradient but can reduce its variance. For a deterministic actor $a=\mu_\theta(x)$, the critic supplies the action derivative and the chain rule gives
\[
\nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{PG}}(\theta)
\approx
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\left.\nabla_a q_\phi(x,a)\right|_{a=\mu_\theta(x)}
\nabla_\theta\mu_\theta(x)
\right].
\]
Actor--critic methods combine these two roles: the critic is updated from TD targets, and the actor is updated using the critic as its learning signal. The actor may represent either a deterministic action-selection rule or a stochastic policy. This structure supports learning policies for the continuous battery-power, mass-flow, and storage-mode setpoints considered in this thesis \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.

\paragraph{On-policy and off-policy learning.}
The on-policy/off-policy distinction concerns the relationship between the policy that generated the data and the policy being improved. Let $\pi_{\mathrm b}$ denote the behavior policy used to collect a transition and $\pi_\theta$ the current target policy being optimized. In on-policy learning, the updates use data generated by the current policy, so $\pi_{\mathrm b}=\pi_\theta$ apart from any exploration mechanism regarded as part of that policy. As the policy changes, older samples become less representative of the current policy.

In off-policy learning, the behavior and target policies may differ. This permits transitions generated by older policies, or by policies with additional exploration noise, to be stored in a replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$ and reused in many minibatch updates. The sampled action $a_t$ in a replay transition was produced by the behavior policy at data-collection time, while the action used in the bootstrapped target is generated from the current actor or a slowly updated target actor, depending on the algorithm. Reusing decorrelated experience improves data efficiency, although the distribution mismatch can make learning less stable and motivates devices such as target networks and conservative critic targets.

\FloatBarrier
\subsubsection{TD3 and SAC}

\paragraph{Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3):}
TD3 represents its deterministic policy by an actor $\mu_\theta:E\rightarrow A$, where $\theta$ denotes the actor parameters. The output $\mu_\theta(x)$ is the action selected in state $x$; it is not an action probability. In terms of the general policy notation $\pi_t(\cdot\mid x)$ introduced above, the deterministic TD3 policy assigns all probability to the single action $a=\mu_\theta(x)$. The state contains the position within the scheduling horizon, so the same actor network can be used at every time step without an explicit time subscript.

TD3 learns two separate action-value approximations, $q_{\phi_1}$ and $q_{\phi_2}$, with critic-parameter vectors $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$. For a transition $(x_t,a_t,r_t(x_t,a_t),x_{t+1},d_t)$ sampled from the replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$, their common learning target is
\[
y_t^{\mathrm{TD3}}=r_t+\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}(1-d_t)
\min_{j\in\{1,2\}}
q_{\bar\phi_j}\!\left(x_{t+1},\mu_{\bar\theta}(x_{t+1})+\zeta\right),
\]
where $d_t=1$ for a terminal transition, bars denote target-network parameters, and $\zeta$ is clipped target-policy smoothing noise. Taking the smaller of the two target estimates reduces the propagation of overestimated action values. Both critics are fitted to this target according to
\[
L(\phi_j)=
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}
\!\left[\left(q_{\phi_j}(x_t,a_t)-y_t^{\mathrm{TD3}}\right)^2\right],
\qquad j\in\{1,2\}.
\]
TD3 further stabilizes learning by updating the actor and target networks less frequently than the critics \cite{fujimoto2018addressing}.
\[
J_{\mathrm{TD3}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}
\left[
q_{\phi_1}\bigl(x,\mu_\theta(x)\bigr)
\right].
\]
Here, $x\sim\mathcal{D}$ is shorthand for sampling the state component $x$ from a transition stored in the replay buffer. The calligraphic replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$ is distinct from the admissible-action set $D_t(x)$. The actor is trained to maximize $J_{\mathrm{TD3}}$ using the first critic $q_{\phi_1}$. The second critic is not redundant: it is used together with the first critic in the minimum that defines the critic target above. The subscript $1$ merely identifies which of the two critics supplies the actor gradient.

\paragraph{Soft Actor-Critic (SAC):}
SAC instead uses a stochastic actor, $\pi_\theta(a\mid x)$, that defines a distribution over continuous actions. As with TD3, the position in the scheduling horizon is included in $x$, so a separate time index is not required on the neural-network policy. In addition to maximizing expected return, SAC includes an entropy term that encourages exploration while training. Like TD3, SAC uses two critics and experience replay; however, its stochastic policy and entropy objective distinguish it from the deterministic TD3 policy \cite{haarnoja2018soft}. For a replay transition, a next action $a_{t+1}^{\prime}\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x_{t+1})$ is sampled from the current policy. The entropy-adjusted critic target is
\[
y_t^{\mathrm{SAC}}=r_t+\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}(1-d_t)\left[
\min_{j\in\{1,2\}}q_{\bar\psi_j}(x_{t+1},a_{t+1}^{\prime})
-\alpha\log\pi_\theta(a_{t+1}^{\prime}\mid x_{t+1})
\right].
\]
The target critics $q_{\bar\psi_1}$ and $q_{\bar\psi_2}$ are slowly updated copies of the learned critics. Each critic minimizes
\[
L(\psi_j)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\left[
\left(q_{\psi_j}(x_t,a_t)-y_t^{\mathrm{SAC}}\right)^2
\right],\qquad j\in\{1,2\}.
\]
The entropy term implements a soft TD target that values both predicted return and policy randomness. At the trajectory level, SAC maximizes an entropy-regularized expected return. In the implemented off-policy algorithm, the actor update is estimated using states from the replay buffer and actions sampled from the current policy:
\[
J_{\mathrm{SAC}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\substack{x\sim\mathcal{D}\\
a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}}\!\left[
\min_{j\in\{1,2\}}q_{\psi_j}(x,a)
-\alpha\log\pi_\theta(a\mid x)
\right].
\]
Here, $x\sim\mathcal{D}$ denotes sampling the state component from a replay-buffer transition, whereas $a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ denotes sampling an action from the current policy for that state. The functions $q_{\psi_1}$ and $q_{\psi_2}$ are the two SAC critics, and their minimum reduces positive estimation bias. The coefficient $\alpha$ weights the entropy contribution. The actor is trained to maximize $J_{\mathrm{SAC}}$; equivalently, implementations written as minimization use the negative of this expression.

For a state $x$, the policy entropy is defined as
\[
\mathcal{H}\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)
= -\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[\log \pi_\theta(a\mid x)\right].
\]
Thus, entropy is an expectation over the policy's action distribution rather than a quantity associated with a single action. During SAC training, an action $a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ is sampled from the current policy. The quantity $-\log\pi_\theta(a\mid x)$ is a stochastic single-sample estimate of the entropy term: although one sample is generally not equal to the exact entropy, its expectation over actions drawn from the policy equals $\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x))$. Such stochastic estimates allow SAC to optimize the entropy-regularized objective using minibatch gradient updates \cite{haarnoja2018soft}.

Both TD3 and SAC are model-free, off-policy actor--critic algorithms. TD3 collects data using its deterministic actor plus exploration noise, but trains from replayed transitions and forms its next action with a target actor. SAC likewise trains its critics and actor from replay-buffer states while sampling target or actor-update actions from its current stochastic policy. Consequently, calling the methods off-policy does not mean that their actor updates ignore the current policy; it means that the stored states and actions used for learning need not have been generated by the policy currently being optimized. Their replay buffers provide the sampled transitions, their critics learn through TD errors, and their actors produce continuous setpoint decisions \cite{fujimoto2018addressing,haarnoja2018soft}.

The two algorithms therefore provide a comparison between deterministic and stochastic actor--critic policies for continuous setpoint optimization. Their performance is evaluated using operating cost, constraint satisfaction, and computation time.

\section{Battery Energy Storage System}
\label{sec:battery_case}

This case study considers a battery energy storage system as the simpler benchmark, with one control command and state-of-charge dynamics. The system model and economic objective are presented first, followed by the reinforcement-learning methodology and numerical results.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:battery_problem}

A battery energy storage system (BESS) is an electrochemical device that stores electrical energy for later use and provides grid services such as energy arbitrage, peak shaving, and short-term balancing. A typical BESS is characterized by its usable energy capacity, maximum charge/discharge power, round-trip efficiency, and state-of-charge (SOC) operating limits. Real-world operation must balance economic objectives (e.g., buying electricity at low prices and selling it at high prices) with engineering constraints (SOC bounds, power limits, efficiency losses).

BESS operation presents several challenges. Decisions are inherently time-coupled because current charging and discharging changes future availability. In addition, uncertain exogenous signals (market prices) require policies that are robust to forecast errors, and hard feasibility constraints must be enforced at every time step. These properties make BESS an illustrative case study for comparing optimization-based and reinforcement-learning approaches to real-time setpoint determination in energy systems.

\begin{figure}[!b]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/battery_model.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic of the battery energy storage system. The battery charges from the grid and sells electricity back to the grid; its state of charge $\mathrm{SOC}_t$ records the stored energy.}
    \label{fig:battery_model}
\end{figure}

A battery energy storage system provides flexible power by buying electricity when prices are low and selling it back to the grid when prices are high. This price-arbitrage operation maximizes profit. A fixed buy--sell price spread $\Delta_s\geq 0$ can be represented by defining the purchase price as $S_t^{\mathrm{buy}}=S_t$ and the selling price as $S_t^{\mathrm{sell}}=S_t-\Delta_s$. In the numerical experiments considered here, $\Delta_s=0$, so the same time-varying electricity price is used for purchases and sales.

The SOC dynamics are modeled as
\[
\mathrm{SOC}_{t+1} = \mathrm{SOC}_t + \frac{P_{\mathrm{actual},t}\,\Delta t_{\mathrm h}}{E_{\max}},
\]
Here, $\mathrm{SOC}_t$ is the state of charge at the beginning of decision step $t$, $\mathrm{SOC}_0$ is the initial state, and $\mathrm{SOC}_N$ is the terminal state after $N$ decisions. Furthermore, $P_{\mathrm{actual},t}$ is the effective storage-side power after efficiency losses, $E_{\max}$ is the usable energy capacity, and $\Delta t_{\mathrm h}$ is the time-step duration in hours. The sign convention is $P_t^{\mathrm S}>0$ for charging and grid purchase, and $P_t^{\mathrm S}<0$ for discharging and electricity sale. The superscript $\mathrm S$ identifies power exchanged with the electricity supply grid. The mathematical formulation uses the following smooth power and terminal constraints:

\begin{align}
P_t^{\mathrm S} &= P_{\max}\Big[
\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh\bigl(k(u_t-\delta)\bigr)\right)u_t
\nonumber\\
&\quad {}\cdot
\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh\bigl(k(0.995-\mathrm{SOC}_t)\bigr)\right)
\nonumber\\
&\quad {}+ \frac{1}{2}\left(1-\tanh\bigl(k(u_t+\delta)\bigr)\right)u_t
\nonumber\\
&\quad {}\cdot
\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh\bigl(k(\mathrm{SOC}_t-0.005)\bigr)\right)
\Big], \quad \forall t,
\label{eq:bess_power_mapping} \\[1ex]
P_{\mathrm{actual},t} &= \frac{P_t^{\mathrm S}}{2}
\Biggl[
\left(1+\tanh\left(\frac{P_t^{\mathrm S}}{\epsilon}\right)\right)\eta
\nonumber\\
&\quad {}+ \left(1-\tanh\left(\frac{P_t^{\mathrm S}}{\epsilon}\right)\right)\frac{1}{\eta}
\Biggr],
\quad \forall t,
\label{eq:feasible_actual_power} \\[1ex]
\mathrm{SOC}_N &\geq \mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{term}},
\qquad \mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{term}}=0.5.
\label{eq:terminal_soc_requirement}
\end{align}

Here, $u_t$ is the normalized control command and $P_{\max}$ is the maximum charging and discharging power. The parameter $k=700$ controls the steepness of the smooth action and SOC gates: increasing $k$ makes their transitions more abrupt, whereas decreasing it produces a wider and more gradual transition. The parameter $\delta$ shifts the charging and discharging gates away from zero and thereby defines a smooth deadband for small control commands. The implemented value $\delta=0.0025$ corresponds to $0.25\%$ of the maximum normalized action magnitude, or a nominal power command of $0.05\,\mathrm{kW}$ for $P_{\max}=20\,\mathrm{kW}$. This small numerical deadband attenuates near-zero commands and reduces switching around zero while leaving the usable action range effectively unchanged.

The parameter $\eta$ describes the charging and discharging efficiency. The power scale $\epsilon=1\,\mathrm{kW}$ determines the width of the smooth transition between the charging and discharging efficiency relations in Eq.~\eqref{eq:feasible_actual_power}. A larger $\epsilon$ produces a more gradual transition around $P_t^{\mathrm S}=0$, while a smaller value more closely approximates an abrupt change between the two operating directions. Equation~\eqref{eq:bess_power_mapping} maps the normalized action and current SOC to grid-side battery power. It progressively restricts charging as SOC approaches $0.995$ and discharging as SOC approaches $0.005$. Equation~\eqref{eq:feasible_actual_power} maps grid-side power to storage-side power through the smooth efficiency relation.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/battery_smooth_mappings.pdf}
    \caption{Visualization of the smooth battery mappings in Eqs.~(\ref{eq:bess_power_mapping}) and~(\ref{eq:feasible_actual_power}). Panel (a) shows grid-side power as a function of the normalized action for $\mathrm{SOC}_t\in\{0.005,0.5,0.995\}$. The black dotted line shows the linear reference $P_t^{\mathrm S}=P_{\max}u_t$. Panel (b) shows the conversion from grid-side to storage-side power. The plotted parameters are $P_{\max}=20\,\mathrm{kW}$, $k=700$, $\delta=0.0025$, $\eta=0.9$, and $\epsilon=1\,\mathrm{kW}$.}
    \label{fig:battery_smooth_mappings}
\end{figure}

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:battery_smooth_mappings}(a), the mapping is approximately $P_t^{\mathrm S}=P_{\max}u_t$ when the SOC is far from its limits and the action lies outside the narrow deadband. At the gate locations $\mathrm{SOC}_t=0.005$ and $\mathrm{SOC}_t=0.995$, discharging and charging power, respectively, are reduced by one half. Figure~\ref{fig:battery_smooth_mappings}(b) illustrates the efficiency relation: away from the transition around zero, positive charging power is multiplied approximately by $\eta$, whereas negative discharging power is multiplied approximately by $1/\eta$. The hyperbolic tangent provides a differentiable transition between these two regimes.

The battery model has a one-dimensional action space at each time step because the scalar command $u_t$ is the only independently selected control variable. Over the complete horizon, the optimization baseline therefore determines one charging or discharging command for each time step, while battery power and SOC follow from the model equations.

The battery objective is to maximize profit. Let $P_t^+=\max(P_t^{\mathrm S},0)$ denote purchased power and $P_t^-=\max(-P_t^{\mathrm S},0)$ denote sold power. Minimizing the net grid-side cost is equivalent to maximizing profit, giving the general objective
\[
\min_{\{u_t\}_{t=0}^{N-1}} J_{\mathrm{grid}}
= \frac{1}{1000}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}
\left(S_t^{\mathrm{buy}}P_t^+-S_t^{\mathrm{sell}}P_t^-\right)\Delta t_{\mathrm h}.
\]
With $S_t^{\mathrm{buy}}=S_t$ and $S_t^{\mathrm{sell}}=S_t-\Delta_s$, the spread reduces the revenue received for exported electricity. The numerical choice $\Delta_s=0$ yields $S_t^{\mathrm{buy}}=S_t^{\mathrm{sell}}=S_t$ and recovers $J_{\mathrm{grid}}=\sum_t S_tP_t^{\mathrm S}\Delta t_{\mathrm h}/1000$. The factor $1000$ converts kWh to MWh so that $J_{\mathrm{grid}}$ is expressed in euros. This equation represents the physical economic objective used to compare the methods. In the optimization baseline formulation, the terminal SOC is imposed as the hard constraint in Eq.~\eqref{eq:terminal_soc_requirement}. In the RL formulation, the negative per-step economic contribution is supplemented by a terminal-SOC penalty and, in an additional experimental variant, a progressive terminal-reachability penalty. These penalties guide policy training but are not included in $J_{\mathrm{grid}}$ because they do not represent physical operating costs; their definitions are given in Section~\ref{sec:battery_methodology}. The battery purchases electricity from the grid when prices are low and sells it back to the grid when prices are high. The main challenges include respecting SOC and power limits, accounting for efficiency losses, handling uncertain future prices, and managing the fact that actions now affect future energy availability. These features make the BESS a suitable test case for reinforcement learning, where a policy must balance using energy now against saving it for likely higher-price periods while always obeying feasibility limits.

\subsection{Methodology}
\label{sec:battery_methodology}

This subsection develops the reinforcement-learning formulation for the battery and establishes the implementation, training setup, and evaluation procedure used for both case studies. The corresponding cookie-factory state, action mapping, and reward are specified in Section~\ref{sec:cookie_methodology}. At each time step, the environment maps the policy action to physical setpoints, advances the system dynamics, and returns the next state, reward, and termination signals. For the optimization baseline, IPOPT solves the finite-horizon battery problem described in Section~\ref{sec:battery_problem}, with the terminal SOC requirement imposed as a hard constraint.

\subsubsection{Reinforcement Learning Formulation}
\label{sec:rl_framework}

For both use cases, the objective is to determine operating setpoints over a discrete time horizon that optimize the relevant economic objective while respecting all constraints. Let $t \in \{0,\ldots,N-1\}$ denote the discrete time index, $S_t$ the spot electricity price, $P_t^{\mathrm S}$ the grid power, and $W_t$ the available wind power. At each time step, the system is described by a state vector $x_t$, the agent selects a continuous action vector $a_t$, and the system evolves to the next state $x_{t+1}$ according to the underlying system dynamics. For the battery case, the only external input is the electricity price $S_t$.

The reinforcement-learning framework used in this thesis follows the same general structure for both use cases. The environments are implemented using Gymnasium \cite{towers2024gymnasium}, while the continuous-control policies are trained using Stable-Baselines3 \cite{raffin2021stablebaselines3}. Each interaction produces the transition sample
\[
\left(x_t,a_t,r_t(x_t,a_t),x_{t+1},d_t\right),
\]
where $d_t$ indicates whether the episode has ended. In the present environments, reaching the end of the fixed scheduling horizon is represented by the \texttt{terminated} signal. The transitions are stored in a replay buffer and sampled repeatedly during training, enabling the off-policy learning used by SAC and TD3.
This implementation follows the general agent--environment interaction illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:rl_interaction_loop}, extended by forecast-augmented states, environment-specific action mappings, and constraint handling.

For the battery system, the state supplied to the agent consists of the current state of charge, a normalized representation of the current spot electricity price $S_t$, the normalized position within the scheduling horizon, and normalized values of the full 24-step price sequence $(S_0,\ldots,S_{N-1})$. Normalization applies to the network observation; $S_t$ in the cost equations retains its physical unit of EUR/MWh. As future work, a rolling-horizon implementation could update the forecast at every decision step. Here, the complete forecast is kept fixed to enable a direct comparison with the optimization baseline over the same single scheduling horizon. The continuous scalar action $u_t\in[-1,1]$ represents the battery charging or discharging command: positive values correspond to charging and negative values to discharging. The battery environment converts $u_t$ into a requested grid-side power using smooth action and SOC gates, which attenuate charging near $\mathrm{SOC}_t=0.995$ and discharging near $\mathrm{SOC}_t=0.005$. A separate storage-side power limit, described below, ensures that the next SOC remains within $[0,1]$.

During training, the policy action is first processed by an environment-specific action map
\[
a_t^{\mathrm{phys}} = \mathcal{M}(x_t,a_t),
\]
where $\mathcal{M}$ maps the policy output to physical setpoints and applies environment-specific feasibility handling. The constraints enforced directly by this map differ between the two use cases.

For the battery, the bounded action $u_t\in[-1,1]$ and the smooth power gates restrict the requested grid-side power. The SOC gates attenuate charging and discharging near the corresponding limits, but do not alone guarantee that the next SOC remains feasible. Therefore, after applying the efficiency mapping, the environment limits the requested storage-side power $\widehat P_{\mathrm{actual},t}$ to the energy available within one time step:
\[
P_{\mathrm{actual},t}
=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
\widehat P_{\mathrm{actual},t},
-\frac{\mathrm{SOC}_t E_{\max}}{\Delta t_{\mathrm h}},
\frac{(1-\mathrm{SOC}_t)E_{\max}}{\Delta t_{\mathrm h}}
\right),
\]
where $\operatorname{clip}(z,l,h)=\min(\max(z,l),h)$. These bounds prevent discharging more energy than is stored or charging beyond the remaining capacity, so the SOC update gives $0\leq\mathrm{SOC}_{t+1}\leq1$. If this limit changes the requested power, the corresponding grid-side power is recomputed through the inverse efficiency mapping before calculating the electricity cost. The terminal SOC requirement is handled by reward penalties.

The reward has the same general form in both environments:
\[
r_t(x_t,a_t) = -C_t - \sum_{\ell} \lambda_\ell v_{\ell,t} - \lambda_{\mathrm{T}} v_{\mathrm{T},t},
\]
where $C_t$ is the grid-electricity cost at time step $t$, $v_{\ell,t}$ are constraint violations, and $\lambda_\ell$ are their corresponding penalty weights. The terminal violation $v_{\mathrm{T},t}$ includes the final-step indicator and is zero before the end of an episode, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{T}}$ is its penalty weight. In the battery case, it represents an insufficient terminal SOC, while in the cookie factory it represents deviation from the cyclic terminal TES temperature. This reward design converts the case-specific economic objective into a return-maximization problem without removing the need for physical feasibility.

More specifically, the battery reward with only a terminal-SOC penalty is
\[
r_t(x_t,a_t) = -\frac{S_t P_t^{\mathrm S}\Delta t_{\mathrm h}}{1000}
-\mathbbm{1}_{\{t=N-1\}}\lambda_{\mathrm{SOC}}
\max\!\left(\mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{term}}-\mathrm{SOC}_N,0\right),
\]
where $S_t$ is the spot electricity price in EUR/MWh, $P_t^{\mathrm S}$ is the grid-side battery power in kW, and $\Delta t_{\mathrm h}$ is the step duration in hours. This implemented expression is the $\Delta_s=0$ special case of the general buy--sell formulation above. Division by $1000$ converts kWh to MWh so that the energy charge is expressed in euros. The terminal term is applied only at the end of an episode and penalizes a final SOC below the prescribed minimum. Intermediate SOC feasibility is enforced by the storage-side power limit described above and the subsequent SOC update.

The correspondence with the general reward is explicit:
\begin{align*}
C_t &= \frac{S_tP_t^{\mathrm S}\Delta t_{\mathrm h}}{1000},
& \sum_\ell\lambda_\ell v_{\ell,t} &= 0,\\
\lambda_{\mathrm T} &= \lambda_{\mathrm{SOC}},
& v_{\mathrm T,t} &= \mathbbm{1}_{\{t=N-1\}}
\max\!\left(\mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{term}}-\mathrm{SOC}_N,0\right).
\end{align*}
Thus, the economic term and the terminal penalty have the same structure in both expressions. The running violation sum is zero for this battery variant because intermediate SOC feasibility is enforced directly. The indicator is included in $v_{\mathrm T,t}$, making the terminal term zero before the final decision step.

As an additional feasibility measure, a progressive terminal-SOC reachability penalty was investigated. At each time step, the environment calculates the minimum SOC required to retain a feasible path to the terminal-SOC target under the maximum possible future charging rate:
\[
\mathrm{SOC}^{\mathrm{reach}}_t
= \max\!\left(
\mathrm{SOC}_{\mathrm{term}}
- (N-t)\frac{\eta P_{\max}\Delta t_{\mathrm h}}{E_{\max}},
0
\right).
\]
Whenever the current SOC falls below this reachability threshold, the shaped reward is
\[
r_t^{\mathrm{reach}}(x_t,a_t)
= r_t(x_t,a_t)
- \lambda_{\mathrm{reach}}
\max\!\left(
\mathrm{SOC}^{\mathrm{reach}}_t-\mathrm{SOC}_t,
0
\right),
\]
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{reach}}>0$ weights the reachability penalty. In the general reward, this additional term corresponds to the violation $v_{\mathrm{reach},t}=\max(\mathrm{SOC}^{\mathrm{reach}}_t-\mathrm{SOC}_t,0)$ with weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{reach}}$. Unlike the terminal penalty described above, this penalty provides feedback before the final time step and encourages the policy to preserve sufficient energy for the terminal requirement. The resulting feasibility--cost trade-off is reported in Section~\ref{sec:battery_results}.

Panel (b) uses the idle action $u_t=0$ at the shown nonterminal steps, for which the baseline reward is $r_t=0$ and hence $r_t^{\mathrm{reach}}=-\lambda_{\mathrm{reach}}v_{\mathrm{reach},t}$. Hollow circles mark the reachability thresholds.}
    \label{fig:battery_reachability_reward}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:battery_reachability_reward} shows that the violation is zero at or above the reachability threshold and increases linearly as SOC falls below it. The shaped reward correspondingly decreases with slope magnitude $\lambda_{\mathrm{reach}}$. As fewer charging steps remain, the threshold rises and the penalty affects a wider range of SOC values. For a nonzero baseline reward, the reachability term is the same downward adjustment to $r_t$.

\subsubsection{Training Setup}
\label{sec:training_setup}

All computations were performed in Python~3.14.1, with Gymnasium~1.2.2 used to implement the reinforcement-learning environments and Stable-Baselines3~2.7.0, based on PyTorch~2.9.1, used to train the RL policies. Experiments were conducted on an AMD Ryzen~7 7840HS CPU with Radeon~780M Graphics.

Training is performed in reinforcement-learning environments before the learned policies are evaluated on fixed test trajectories. The environment provides the transition dynamics, cost calculation, constraint handling, and terminal condition for each episode. The learning algorithm only receives observations, rewards, and termination signals through the environment interface.

For both use cases, one episode corresponds to a 24-step scheduling horizon. During training, the start index of the episode is sampled randomly from the available time-series data. This random-window strategy exposes the agent to different price and renewable-generation patterns instead of repeatedly starting each episode at the same time of day. Both environments use the grid-price series; the cookie factory environment additionally samples the corresponding wind-availability values for the same 24-hour window.

The training environments are wrapped with monitoring and vectorized environment interfaces. Monitoring records episode rewards and lengths for later inspection, while the vectorized wrapper provides the interface expected by the Stable-Baselines3 implementation of SAC and TD3. Only one environment instance is used at a time in the current setup, so the vectorization is mainly an interface layer rather than a parallel simulation strategy.

The environment-specific action mappings and reward definitions are given in
Sections~\ref{sec:rl_framework} and~\ref{sec:cookie_rl_framework}, including the cookie-factory reward in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_reward}. The cookie-factory penalties are particularly important because
its coupled nonlinear feasibility requirements cannot all be represented by
simple action bounds.

Hyperparameter selection included 50 tuning trials. The preliminary battery learning-rate study used to select the linear schedule is reported in Section~\ref{sec:battery_results}. The final training settings, which are shared by the battery and cookie-factory case studies, are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:sac_training_setup}. The environments themselves still differ in their state variables, action spaces, dynamics, and constraint structures.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Procedure}
\label{sec:evaluation_metrics}

The trained RL policies are evaluated through deterministic rollouts on fixed 24-hour evaluation windows. For SAC, the mean action of the stochastic policy is selected at each time step. TD3 directly outputs a deterministic action. This produces one reproducible setpoint trajectory for a given price and wind profile and allows direct comparison with the optimization baseline in terms of cost, constraint satisfaction, and computation time.

The primary economic metric is the total net grid-side cost over the evaluation horizon:
\[
J_{\mathrm{grid}} = \sum_{t=0}^{N-1} C_t,
\]
where $C_t$ is the grid-electricity cost at time step $t$. For the battery system, this cost may generally include the fixed buy--sell spread $\Delta_s$ defined in Section~\ref{sec:battery_problem}; the numerical experiments use $\Delta_s=0$. Minimizing $J_{\mathrm{grid}}$ is equivalent to maximizing profit. For the cookie factory, the cost is computed from the grid power required by the heat-pump system after accounting for available wind power and is minimized directly. Thus, a lower value of $J_{\mathrm{grid}}$ indicates higher profit for the battery and lower operating cost for the cookie factory.

The cumulative reward is reported as a secondary metric:
\[
R_{\mathrm{ep}} = \sum_{t=0}^{N-1} r_t(x_t,a_t)
= -J_{\mathrm{grid}}
- \sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\left(\sum_{\ell}\lambda_\ell v_{\ell,t}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm T}v_{\mathrm T,t}\right).
\]
When all penalty terms are zero, the cumulative episode reward equals the negative total grid-electricity cost, $R_{\mathrm{ep}}=-J_{\mathrm{grid}}$. Otherwise, it also reflects penalties for constraint violations.

Because $\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}=1$ and the terminal penalty is included in the reward returned with the final transition, this cumulative episode reward contains the same economic and penalty contributions as the realized return $G_0$.
This value is useful for analyzing the RL objective because it combines the economic term with penalty terms. However, it is not used as the only comparison criterion, since the reward may include artificial penalty weights that are not part of the physical economic objective. Therefore, the economic objective and constraint satisfaction are evaluated separately.

Constraint satisfaction is measured separately for each constraint category. For a nonnegative residual magnitude or violation $v_{\ell,t}$ in category $\ell$, the total violation over one rollout is defined as
\[
V_{\mathrm{tot},\ell} = \sum_{t=0}^{N-1} v_{\ell,t}.
\]
Each $V_{\mathrm{tot},\ell}$ retains the unit of its category; violations with different units are not added together. In addition, the fraction of time steps with at least one violation is considered:
\[
f_{\mathrm{viol}} =
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}
\mathbbm{1}_{\{\exists\,\ell:\;v_{\ell,t} > \varepsilon_{\mathrm{viol},\ell}\}},
\]
where $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{viol},\ell}$ is a small numerical tolerance expressed in the unit of category $\ell$. For the battery system, the relevant checks include SOC bounds, power limits, and the terminal SOC requirement. For the cookie factory, the violation categories include bypass feasibility, complementarity of charging and discharging, TES temperature bounds, grid-power bounds, coupling residuals, and the terminal cyclic TES condition.

The evaluation also records trajectory-level operating indicators. For the BESS, these include the SOC trajectory, commanded and effective battery power, and terminal SOC.

Finally, computation time is used to assess practical applicability. For the optimization baseline, the relevant value is the solver time required to compute a complete schedule for the evaluation horizon. For RL, the one-time training effort is reported separately from the online evaluation time. This distinction is important because RL can require substantial training effort, but a trained policy can usually produce setpoints much faster than solving a nonlinear optimization problem from scratch for every horizon.

Offline training time is excluded from all online computation time comparisons.

\label{sec:battery_results}


A preliminary learning-rate study was conducted for SAC using the battery environment. A constant learning rate, a linear-decay schedule, and a power-law-decay schedule were each trained and evaluated over 32 independent runs using the same evaluation setup. \section{Cookie Production Factory}
\label{sec:cookie_case}

The second case study considers the cookie production factory as the more complex benchmark, combining multiple controllable components, nonlinear process behavior, thermal energy storage, and coupled operational constraints. As in the battery case, the problem formulation is followed by the methodology and numerical results.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:cookie_problem}

The second use case represents a nonlinear cookie production factory with an electrified steam-generation utility system. The production process requires a constant supply of process heat in the form of superheated steam, while the utility system must decide how to operate the energy conversion components under fluctuating wind generation $W_t$ and electricity prices $S_t$. The operational objective is to satisfy the steam demand while minimizing grid electricity cost over a finite scheduling horizon. The system model is based on the industrial power-to-heat system introduced by Walden et al.~\cite{walden2023nonlinear} and later used as a renewable steam-generation benchmark by Kyriakidis et al.~\cite{kyriakidis2024hybrid}.

The system consists of four main units: a wind turbine (WT), a closed reverse Brayton-cycle high-temperature heat pump (HTHP), a sensible thermal energy storage (TES), and a steam generator (SG). The WT supplies renewable electricity, while additional electricity can be purchased from the grid. The HTHP uses a waste-heat air stream as its heat source and delivers high-temperature process heat to an intermediate heat-transfer-fluid (HTF) loop. The HTF can be routed through the TES or directly to the SG by controllable bypasses. The SG provides the required steam conditions for the consumer factory. The HTHP and SG are represented by polynomial surrogate models, while the TES is modeled as a simplified single energy reservoir, and the charging/discharging behavior is represented using efficiency equations. Figure~\ref{fig:cookie_steam_system} provides an overview of the complete system, while Fig.~\ref{fig:cookie_detailed_flow_chart} shows the detailed HTF flow paths and bypass variables.

Let $N$ be the number of decision steps in the scheduling horizon, with
$t\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}$ for controls and stream quantities and
$t\in\{0,\ldots,N\}$ for the TES state. The objective $J_{\mathrm{grid}}$
is the total grid-electricity cost in EUR. The spot electricity price $S_t$
is in EUR/MWh, the purchased grid power $P_t^{\mathrm S}$ and available wind
power $W_t$ are in kW, and $\Delta t_{\mathrm h}$ is the step duration in
hours. The factor $1/1000$ converts kWh to MWh in the objective. The duration
$\Delta t_{\mathrm s}=3600\Delta t_{\mathrm h}$ in seconds is used in the
thermal-storage balance. The five independently selected quantities are the
bypass settings $\beta_{1,t}$ and $\beta_{2,t}$, the dimensionless
compressor-speed-related variable $R_t$, the mass flow
$\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}$ in kg/s, and the HTHP inlet temperature
$T_{\mathrm{I},t}$ in ${}^\circ\mathrm C$.

In compound subscripts such as those in $T_{\mathrm{I},t}$ and $T_{1,t}$, the
Roman labels $\mathrm I$--$\mathrm{IV}$ and Arabic labels $1$--$4$ identify
physical locations in the HTF circuit shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cookie_detailed_flow_chart}, whereas the final subscript $t$ is the time index. All temperatures are in ${}^\circ\mathrm C$.
$T_{\mathrm{I},t}$ and $T_{\mathrm{II},t}$ are respectively the inlet and outlet
temperatures of the high-temperature heat exchanger (HTHX), whereas
$T_{\mathrm{III},t}$ and $T_{\mathrm{IV},t}$ are the inlet and outlet
temperatures of the low-temperature heat exchanger (LTHX). The temperatures
$T_{1,t}$ and $T_{4,t}$ are the TES outlet temperatures during charging and
discharging, respectively, while $T_{2,t}$ and $T_{3,t}$ are the steam-generator
inlet and outlet temperatures. Thus, $T_{\mathrm{I},t}$ is the HTHX inlet
temperature at step $t$, while $T_{1,t}$ is the TES charging outlet
temperature at the same step. The quantities $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}$ and
$\dot{m}_{\mathrm{II},t}$ denote the HTHX inlet and outlet HTF mass-flow
rates. The implemented closed-loop model uses the same per-HTHP flow variable
for these two locations, so $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{II},t}=\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}$. The functions $F_{\mathrm{HTHP}}$,
$F_{\mathrm{HTHX}}$, and $F_{\mathrm{LTHX}}$ are polynomial surrogate models
for the electrical demand of one HTHP, the HTHX outlet temperature, and the
LTHX outlet temperature, respectively.

The bypass settings $\beta_{1,t},\beta_{2,t}\in[0,1]$ determine the TES operating
mode. A value of one fully bypasses the associated storage path: charging uses
$\beta_{1,t}<1$ and $\beta_{2,t}=1$, discharging uses $\beta_{2,t}<1$ and
$\beta_{1,t}=1$, and $\beta_{1,t}=\beta_{2,t}=1$ represents idle operation.
$\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{ch},t}$ and $\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{dch},t}$ are the TES charging
and discharging heat-flow rates in kW. The parameter
$c_{p,\mathrm f}$ is the specific heat capacity of the HTF in kJ/(kg\,K), while
$\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}$ and $\epsilon_{\mathrm{dch}}$ are the charging and
discharging effectiveness factors, both dimensionless. The small relaxation
parameter $\gamma$, in $\mathrm{kW}^2$, bounds the product of the charging
and discharging heat rates. Choosing it close to zero makes the two flows
approximately mutually exclusive.

Finally, $T_{s,t}$ is the TES temperature at the beginning of decision step $t$,
and $T_{s,t+1}$ is the updated temperature after applying the decision at that
step. The parameters $m_s$ and $c_{p,\mathrm s}$ denote the storage-medium mass
in kg and specific heat capacity in kJ/(kg\,K), respectively. The prescribed
initial temperature is
$\tilde T_0$, so $T_{s,0}=\tilde T_0$, and $T_{s,N}=T_{s,0}$ imposes cyclic terminal
operation.

\begin{samepage}
The corresponding finite-horizon optimization problem is formulated over a discrete time horizon with time index $t \in \{0,\ldots,N-1\}$:
\begin{align}
&\min_{\{\beta_{1,t},\beta_{2,t},R_t,\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t},T_{\mathrm{I},t}\}_{t=0}^{N-1}}
\quad J_{\mathrm{grid}} = \frac{1}{1000}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1} P_t^{\mathrm S} S_t \Delta t_{\mathrm h},
\label{eq:cookie_objective} \\
&\text{s.t.} \quad P_t^{\mathrm S} + W_t = 3F_{\mathrm{HTHP}}\left(T_{\mathrm{I},t},\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t},T_{\mathrm{III},t},R_t\right), \label{eq:cookie_power_balance} \\
&T_{\mathrm{II},t} = F_{\mathrm{HTHX}}\left(T_{\mathrm{I},t},\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t},T_{\mathrm{III},t},R_t\right), \\
&T_{\mathrm{IV},t} = F_{\mathrm{LTHX}}\left(T_{\mathrm{I},t},\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t},T_{\mathrm{III},t},R_t\right), \\
&T_{2,t} = \beta_{1,t} T_{\mathrm{II},t} + \left(1-\beta_{1,t}\right)T_{1,t}, \label{eq:cookie_charging_coupling} \\
&T_{\mathrm{I},t} = \beta_{2,t} T_{3,t} + \left(1-\beta_{2,t}\right)T_{4,t}, \label{eq:cookie_discharging_mixing} \\
&\dot{m}_{\mathrm{II},t} = \dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}, \\
&T_{2,t} = 201.92 + \frac{1819.32}{3\dot{m}_{\mathrm{II},t}}, \\
&T_{3,t} = 196.3 - \frac{188.4}{3\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}}, \\
&\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{ch},t} = 3\dot{m}_{\mathrm{II},t} c_{p,\mathrm{f}}\left(T_{\mathrm{II},t}-T_{1,t}\right)\left(1-\beta_{1,t}\right), \\
&\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{dch},t} = 3\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t} c_{p,\mathrm{f}}\left(T_{4,t}-T_{3,t}\right)\left(1-\beta_{2,t}\right), \\
&\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{ch},t} \dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{dch},t} \leq \gamma, \\
&T_{1,t} = T_{\mathrm{II},t} - \epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}\left(T_{\mathrm{II},t}-T_{s,t}\right), \\
&T_{4,t} = T_{3,t} - \epsilon_{\mathrm{dch}}\left(T_{3,t}-T_{s,t}\right), \\
&T_{s,t+1} = T_{s,t} + \frac{\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{ch},t}-\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{dch},t}}{m_s c_{p,\mathrm{s}}}\Delta t_{\mathrm s}, \\
&T_{s,0} = \tilde{T}_0, \qquad T_{s,N} = T_{s,0}.
\end{align}
\end{samepage}
The experimental problem also imposes the following operating bounds and
fixed inlet temperature. All temperatures are in ${}^\circ\mathrm C$, powers
and heat rates in $\mathrm{kW}$, and mass-flow rates in $\mathrm{kg/s}$.
The stream and control constraints apply at every decision step
$t\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}$; the TES state bound includes both the initial and
terminal states:
\begin{align}
&0 \leq P_t^{\mathrm S} \leq 5000, \\
&0 \leq \dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{ch},t},\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{dch},t} \leq 5000, \\
&T_{\mathrm{I},t} \in [177,250], \qquad
  \dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t} \in [5,16], \qquad R_t \in [0.8,1.53], \\
&\beta_{1,t},\beta_{2,t} \in [0,1], \qquad
  \beta_{1,t}+\beta_{2,t} \geq 1, \\
&T_{\mathrm{III},t} = 75, \\
&T_{\mathrm{II},t} \in [239,400], \qquad
  T_{\mathrm{IV},t} \in [-60,90], \\
&T_{1,t},T_{4,t} \in [183,332], \\
&T_{2,t} \in [239,324], \qquad T_{3,t} \in [183,193], \\
&183 \leq T_{s,t} \leq 324, \qquad t\in\{0,\ldots,N\}.
\label{eq:cookie_bounds}
\end{align}
The LTHX inlet is fixed at $T_{\mathrm{III},t}=75\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ in
these experiments. The interval $[60,100]\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ describes the
surrogate's validity range for this input, rather than a variable operating
range in the experimental problem.
The label $F_4$ denotes the charging-side coupling relation in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_charging_coupling}, and $F_5$ denotes the
discharging-side mixing relation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_discharging_mixing}.
These are fixed relation labels; the subscript $t$ on each physical variable
denotes time.
The final bound relations specify the admissible operating ranges
and fixed input of the experimental model. The power balance in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_power_balance} and the remaining relations through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_bounds} collectively describe the surrogate components,
bypass mixing, TES dynamics, mutually exclusive charging and discharging,
cyclic operation, and admissible operating ranges. All symbols and their units
are also collected in the nomenclature.

% The main control decisions are the bypass variables $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$, the compressor-speed-related variable $R$, the mass flow rate $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I}}$, and the HTHP inlet temperature $T_{\mathrm{I}}$. The bypass variables determine the operating mode of the system. In charging mode, heat from the HTHP is routed into the TES; in discharging mode, stored heat is released to support the steam-generation process; and in idle or bypass operation, the storage is not actively charged or discharged. Simultaneous charging and discharging is prohibited through a complementarity constraint.


The cookie-factory optimization model has a five-dimensional action space at each time step. Its independently selected operating variables are the two bypass variables $\beta_{1,t}$ and $\beta_{2,t}$, the compressor-speed-related variable $R_t$, the mass flow rate $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}$, and the HTHP inlet temperature $T_{\mathrm{I},t}$. The remaining temperatures, power flows, and storage quantities are coupled through the model equations. In the RL formulation, the action space is reduced to two dimensions: the policy selects a signed bypass command $b_t$ and the mass flow rate $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}$. The environment maps $b_t$ to the two physical bypass coefficients $\beta_{1,t}$ and $\beta_{2,t}$ and derives $R_t$ from the $F_4$ coupling equation. This use case is more complex than the battery system because it combines nonlinear surrogate equations, thermal storage dynamics, and hard process constraints. It therefore provides a suitable second benchmark for evaluating whether reinforcement learning can learn feasible and cost-effective setpoint policies for industrial energy utility systems, and for comparing the learned policy against the optimization baseline.

\subsection{Methodology}
\label{sec:cookie_methodology}

The cookie-factory optimization problem is reformulated as a finite-horizon MDP using the same agent--environment interaction structure introduced in Section~\ref{sec:rl_framework}. Its observation, reduced action space, constraint handling, and reward are defined below. For the optimization baseline, IPOPT solves the finite-horizon problem in Section~\ref{sec:cookie_problem}, including the process constraints and cyclic terminal-storage requirement.

\subsubsection{Reinforcement Learning Formulation}
\label{sec:cookie_rl_framework}

For the cookie factory, the observation contains the normalized TES
temperature, current electricity price and wind availability, the previous
operating mode, the previously applied compressor-speed variable and $F_4$
residual, the position within the episode, the terminal-temperature gap, the
previous action, and the complete price and wind forecasts. The policy action
is reduced to
\[
a_t=\left(b_t,\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}\right),
\qquad b_t\in[-1,1],\quad \dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}\in[5,16],
\]
where $b_t$ is a dimensionless signed bypass command, implemented as
\texttt{beta\_command}. Outside the idle band, its sign selects charging or
discharging, while its distance from the band determines the active bypass
coefficient. The
environment maps this single command to the two physical bypass coefficients
$\beta_{1,t}$ and $\beta_{2,t}$. The idle band has a half-width of
$\delta_\beta=0.05$. This value was selected as a small numerical tolerance
around zero. It suppresses mode switching caused by small variations in the
policy output while retaining most of the normalized action range for active
charging and discharging. The value was fixed during the experiments and was
not separately optimized. The environment maps $b_t$ to the two physical
bypass coefficients according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_beta_mapping}:
\begin{equation}
\left(\beta_{1,t},\beta_{2,t}\right)=
\left\{\begin{array}{@{}l@{\quad}l@{\quad}l@{}}
\displaystyle\left(1,\frac{1+b_t}{1-\delta_\beta}\right),
    & -1\leq b_t<-\delta_\beta & \text{(discharging)},\\[2mm]
(1,1),
    & |b_t|\leq\delta_\beta & \text{(idle)},\\[1mm]
\displaystyle\left(\frac{1-b_t}{1-\delta_\beta},1\right),
    & \delta_\beta<b_t\leq1 & \text{(charging)}.
\end{array}\right.
\label{eq:cookie_beta_mapping}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the inactive path always has a bypass value of one. The mapping
therefore satisfies $\beta_{1,t}+\beta_{2,t}\geq1$ and prevents simultaneous TES
charging and discharging without requiring the policy to learn these two
conditions from reward penalties.

The policy selects only $b_t$ and $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}$; the environment
calculates $R_t$ from the resulting bypass settings and the current TES
temperature $T_{s,t}$. First, using
$\dot{m}_{\mathrm{II},t}=\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}$, it evaluates
\begin{align*}
T_{2,t} &= 201.92+\frac{1819.32}{3\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}},\\
T_{3,t} &= 196.3-\frac{188.4}{3\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}},\\
T_{4,t} &= T_{3,t}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{dch}}(T_{3,t}-T_{s,t}),\\
T_{\mathrm{I},t} &= \beta_{2,t}T_{3,t}+(1-\beta_{2,t})T_{4,t}.
\end{align*}
These steps determine the steam-generator temperatures and the HTHX inlet
temperature without requiring $R_t$.

Next, the charging-side coupling relation $F_4$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_charging_coupling} requires
$T_{2,t}=\beta_{1,t}T_{\mathrm{II},t}+(1-\beta_{1,t})T_{1,t}$.
Substituting
$T_{1,t}=(1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}})T_{\mathrm{II},t}
+\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}T_{s,t}$ and collecting terms gives
\[
T_{2,t}=
(1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}\beta_{1,t})
T_{\mathrm{II},t}
+\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}(1-\beta_{1,t})T_{s,t}.
\]
Solving this linear relation for $T_{\mathrm{II},t}$ gives the required
outlet temperature in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_required_TII}:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{II,req},t}=
\frac{T_{2,t}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}(1-\beta_{1,t})T_{s,t}}
{1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}\beta_{1,t}}.
\label{eq:cookie_required_TII}
\end{equation}
With the implemented $\epsilon_{\mathrm{ch}}=0.9$ and
$\beta_{1,t}\in[0,1]$, the denominator is at least $0.1$, so this inversion
is well defined. The environment then chooses $R_t$ so that the HTHX
surrogate produces this required temperature:
\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{HTHX}}\!\left(
T_{\mathrm{I},t},\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t},T_{\mathrm{III},t},R_t
\right)-T_{\mathrm{II,req},t}=0,
\qquad R_t\in[0.8,1.53].
\label{eq:cookie_R_derivation}
\end{equation}
For the fixed inputs $T_{\mathrm{I},t}$, $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}$, and
$T_{\mathrm{III},t}$, collecting powers of $R$ in the HTHX surrogate gives
\[
F_{\mathrm{HTHX}}(T_{\mathrm{I},t},\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t},T_{\mathrm{III},t},R)
=\sum_{j=0}^{3}\kappa_{j,t}R^j,
\]
where $\kappa_{j,t}$ are the resulting coefficients at time $t$.
Here, $R$ is a trial value at the fixed time step; $R_t$ denotes the value
eventually applied.
Equation~\eqref{eq:cookie_R_derivation} therefore becomes the scalar cubic
\[
\kappa_{3,t}R_t^3+\kappa_{2,t}R_t^2+\kappa_{1,t}R_t
+\kappa_{0,t}-T_{\mathrm{II,req},t}=0.
\]
The environment computes its roots numerically and retains real roots in
$[0.8,1.53]$. When several such roots exist, it selects the root closest to
the previously applied value $R_{t-1}$ for $t\geq1$. At $t=0$, the interval
midpoint $R_{\mathrm{ref}}=1.165$ is used as the reference.
The applied initial value $R_0$ is still obtained from the cubic equation.
Selecting the nearest root minimizes $|R_t-R_{t-1}|$ only among the
available bounded real roots. It is a local continuity heuristic and does
not impose a bound on this change. If the previously selected branch no
longer provides a bounded real root, $R_t$ may change abruptly. Switching
between TES charging and discharging modes is determined by $b_t$ and is
not prevented by this root-selection rule.

If no real root satisfies the bounds, the environment evaluates the absolute
surrogate mismatch at both interval endpoints and at all real stationary
points within the interval. The stationary points satisfy
\[
3\kappa_{3,t}R^2+2\kappa_{2,t}R+\kappa_{1,t}=0.
\]
It applies the candidate with the smallest absolute mismatch, using distance
to the reference value to break ties. This fallback preserves the bounds on
$R_t$ while leaving a possible coupling violation. After selecting $R_t$,
the environment recomputes $T_{\mathrm{II},t}$ and $T_{1,t}$ and measures
the remaining mismatch with the residual of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_charging_coupling}, labeled $F_4$:
\[
v_{F_4,t}=\left|
T_{2,t}-\beta_{1,t}T_{\mathrm{II},t}
-(1-\beta_{1,t})T_{1,t}
\right|.
\]

For the cookie factory, the environment clips $b_t$ and $\dot m_{\mathrm I,t}$ to their allowed intervals and maps $b_t$ to bypass values in $[0,1]$. The inactive storage path always has a bypass value of one, which guarantees $\beta_{1,t}+\beta_{2,t}\geq1$ and prevents simultaneous charging and discharging. The derived compressor-speed variable is also restricted to $R_t\in[0.8,1.53]$. TES and inlet-temperature bounds, heat-flow and grid-power bounds, any remaining $F_4$ coupling residual, and the cyclic terminal-temperature requirement are represented by the reward penalties defined below. These penalties guide learning but do not guarantee constraint satisfaction.

\begin{samepage}
The cookie-factory reward contains the economic cost and the constraints that
are not guaranteed by the action mapping. Define the positive-part operator by
$[\,\cdot\,]_+=\max(\,\cdot\,,0)$,
$\Delta T_s=T_s^{\max}-T_s^{\min}$, and the violations
\begin{align*}
v_{s,t}={}&[T_s^{\min}-T_{s,t+1}]_+
          +[T_{s,t+1}-T_s^{\max}]_+,\\
v_{\mathrm I,t}={}&[177-T_{\mathrm{I},t}]_+
          +[T_{\mathrm{I},t}-250]_+,\\
v_{Q,t}={}&[-\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{ch},t}]_+
          +[-\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{dch},t}]_+
          +[\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{ch},t}-\dot Q_s^{\max}]_+
          +[\dot{Q}_{s,\mathrm{dch},t}-\dot Q_s^{\max}]_+,\\
v_{P,t}={}&[-P_t^{\mathrm S}]_+
          +[P_t^{\mathrm S}-P_{\mathrm S}^{\max}]_+.
\end{align*}
\end{samepage}
Here, $v_{s,t}$ measures violations of the TES temperature bounds,
$v_{\mathrm I,t}$ the HTHX inlet-temperature bounds, $v_{Q,t}$ the TES
charging and discharging heat-flow bounds, and $v_{P,t}$ the grid-power bounds.
Each term is zero when its corresponding constraint is satisfied.
For the one-hour time steps used here, the grid cost is
$C_t=S_tP_t^{\mathrm S}/1000$. The implemented reward is
\begin{align}
r_t^{\mathrm{cookie}}(x_t,a_t)={}&-C_t
-\frac{\lambda_s}{\Delta T_s}v_{s,t}
-\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm I}}{250-177}v_{\mathrm I,t}
-\frac{\lambda_Q}{\dot Q_s^{\max}}v_{Q,t}
-\frac{\lambda_{F_4}}{\Delta T_s}v_{F_4,t}
\nonumber\\
&-\frac{\lambda_P}{P_{\mathrm S}^{\max}}v_{P,t}
-\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}}}{\Delta T_s}v_{\mathrm T,t}.
\label{eq:cookie_reward}
\end{align}
Here, $v_{\mathrm T,t}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{t=N-1\}}|T_{s,N}-T_{s,0}|$ is the
terminal violation. The coefficients multiplying the violations in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_reward} are the corresponding penalty weights in the
general reward.
The values used in training are $T_s^{\min}=183\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$,
$T_s^{\max}=324\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$,
$\dot Q_s^{\max}=5000\,\mathrm{kW}$,
$P_{\mathrm S}^{\max}=5000\,\mathrm{kW}$,
$\lambda_s=20$, $\lambda_{\mathrm I}=10$, $\lambda_Q=5000$,
$\lambda_{F_4}=500$, $\lambda_P=3000$, and
$\lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}}=3000$. These coefficients were selected empirically
during training. The comparatively large
$\lambda_Q$ and $\lambda_P$ strongly discourage heat-flow and grid-power
violations. The terminal coefficient $\lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}}$ is also large
because its penalty is applied only once, at the end of the episode. The smaller $\lambda_s$ and
$\lambda_{\mathrm I}$ reflect that the storage-state and inlet-temperature
bounds were largely satisfied during preliminary runs. These values are fixed
engineering choices rather than proven optimal coefficients. Thus, the physical
operating cost remains identifiable within the reward, while the
penalties provide training feedback for state, inlet-temperature, heat-flow,
$F_4$-coupling, grid-power, and terminal-cyclic violations.

\subsubsection{Training and Evaluation Setup}

The cookie-factory policies use the training procedure in Section~\ref{sec:training_setup} and the shared SAC and TD3 settings in Table~\ref{tab:sac_training_setup}. Each episode spans 24 hourly steps, with matched electricity-price and wind-power windows sampled during training. The linear learning-rate schedule selected in the battery study is also used here.

The policies are evaluated using the procedure and metrics in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_metrics}. SAC uses the mean policy action and TD3 uses its deterministic action, with the same electricity-price and wind-power data supplied to the policies and the optimization baseline. The physical grid-electricity objective in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cookie_objective} is reported separately from reward penalties. Constraint violations are reported by category in their original units, including the $F_4$ coupling residual, heat-flow and grid-power bounds, and terminal cyclic-storage deviation. Offline training effort and online schedule-generation time are reported separately.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion_future_work}

\subsection{Conclusion}

This thesis investigated whether reinforcement learning can determine feasible and cost-effective operating setpoints for energy utility systems of different complexity. The setpoint-selection tasks were formulated as finite-horizon Markov decision processes and evaluated for a battery energy storage system and the electrified steam-generation utility system of a cookie production factory. The battery represents a one-dimensional storage-control problem, whereas the cookie-factory optimization model contains five coupled control variables and nonlinear process constraints. For the cookie-factory RL formulation, the action space was reduced to two dimensions by allowing the policy to select a signed bypass command $b_t$ and the mass flow rate $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{I},t}$. The environment maps $b_t$ to the physical bypass coefficients $\beta_{1,t}$ and $\beta_{2,t}$ and derives the remaining quantities through the process equations. In both cases, the trained policies were compared with the optimization baseline using the original economic objective, constraint satisfaction, and online computation time.

For the battery system, the optimization baseline achieved the highest mean profit, but the TD3 policy approached its performance closely. Across 1000 randomly sampled 24-hour windows, the mean objective was $-3.02$~EUR for TD3 and $-3.19$~EUR for the optimization baseline, giving a mean gap of 0.16~EUR. TD3 also produced SOC trajectories that used a similar portion of the available storage range. Its main advantage was speed: a complete policy rollout required 0.0082~s on average, compared with 0.5605~s for the optimization baseline, corresponding to a speedup of approximately $68.13\times$.

The cookie factory presented a more difficult control problem because nonlinear surrogate equations, storage dynamics, and process constraints must be satisfied simultaneously. Across the 200 matched comparisons with the optimization baseline, SAC produced a mean cost gap of 80.33~EUR and a mean absolute relative gap of 6.31\%, while TD3 produced a smaller mean gap of 69.11~EUR and a smaller mean absolute relative gap of 5.66\%. SAC and TD3 generated schedules in 0.0217~s and 0.0180~s on average, corresponding to speedups of approximately $533.81\times$ and $645.06\times$, respectively. TD3 is therefore selected as the better cookie-factory RL policy overall based on its economic performance, online speed, and smaller grid-power violation. However, SAC yielded smaller $F_4$ residuals, heat-flow-bound violations, and terminal cyclic deviations. Both policies remained substantially less feasible than the optimization baseline.

These online speed advantages required offline training. Training the battery policies required 36 minutes for SAC and 23 minutes for TD3, while training the cookie-factory policies required 2.81 hours and 1.24 hours, respectively.

The results therefore provide a qualified answer to the research question. Reinforcement learning can learn continuous setpoint policies that produce economically meaningful schedules with very low online computation time. Its practical value is strongest when decisions must be generated repeatedly under short computation time limits and when offline training can be performed in advance. In the simpler battery system, the learned policy remained close to the optimization baseline. As system complexity and constraint coupling increased, the gap in operating cost and feasibility became more pronounced. The optimization baseline remained superior when the best objective value and reliable constraint satisfaction were the primary requirements.

\subsection{Future Work}

Constraint handling should be strengthened for the cookie-factory policy. In particular, the observed grid-power violations should be addressed through a constraint-preserving action mapping or action projection. The two-dimensional action formulation should also be investigated further to determine how the derived compressor-speed calculation and bypass-command mapping affect feasibility, sensitivity, and learning stability.

The present evaluation uses fixed 24-hour price and wind profiles that are available to both methods. A rolling-horizon implementation should instead update forecasts and setpoints as new measurements become available. This would allow the policies to be tested under forecast errors, measurement noise, disturbances, and model--plant mismatch. Domain randomization or targeted retraining could then be used to improve robustness to operating conditions not represented in the original training data.

The comparison with the optimization baseline can also be extended. The schedule generated by the RL policy could be used to warm-start IPOPT, while hybrid methods such as BO-IPOPT should be evaluated on the same windows and computation budgets. Such hybrid approaches could use rapidly generated RL setpoints to initialize a subsequent optimization step that restores feasibility and improves the objective. Finally, the proposed policies should be validated using a higher-fidelity real-plant implementation.
